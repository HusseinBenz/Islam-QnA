Imagine two people bring the same verse to settle a dispute, and each gives a key word a different meaning. For the verse to resolve anything, there must be some way to **compare their readings** and say which fits the evidence better. If every reading were equally valid, scripture could never correct anyone. It could only reflect back whatever each reader brought to it.

## The Qur'an claims to be clear

The Qur'an describes itself as *"a clear Arabic Qur'an"* (12:2; 16:103), revealed *"in a clear Arabic tongue"* (26:195), and *"made clear"* in its verses (11:1). It also warns against those who *"distort words from their places"* (4:46). These claims assume that meaning is not infinitely flexible.

## How meaning is established

Ibn Taymiyya's *Muqaddima fi Usul al-Tafsir* (*Introduction to the Principles of Commentary*, in *Majmu' al-Fatawa*, vol. 13) sets out the classical order of evidence for interpreting the Qur'an:

1. **The Qur'an itself.** What one passage states briefly, another explains.
2. **The Sunnah.** The Prophet was sent to explain the revelation (16:44).
3. **The Companions.** They witnessed the revelation and its context.
4. **Arabic usage.** The language as it was spoken when the Qur'an was revealed.

Al-Shatibi adds in *al-Muwafaqat* that the law must be understood according to the established usage of the Arabs, not according to meanings a word acquired later or in another language. Al-Tabari's commentary, which records the early explanations with their chains, remains the first stop for this kind of evidence.

Sometimes these sources leave **several readings** open, and classical scholars recorded those differences honestly. In other cases the evidence **narrows** the meaning, and some proposed readings simply do not fit.

## A test case: the meaning of *zina*

The modern Syrian writer Muhammad Shahrur proposed narrowing *zina*, traditionally "unlawful sexual intercourse," to mean only open or public sexual misconduct, or infidelity within marriage. Private consensual relations between unmarried people would fall outside it. The proposal is worth testing on its merits, because it shows how such questions can be decided.

Does the restriction explain how the Qur'an uses the term and its related vocabulary?

- **Qur'an 24:2** prescribes a penalty for *"the woman and the man who commit zina."* It does not limit this to married people. Classical jurists applied the flogging precisely to the unmarried.
- **Qur'an 4:25** describes the women whom men may marry as *"chaste, not committing unlawful intercourse, and not taking secret lovers"* (*muhsanat ghayra musafihat wa-la muttakhidhat akhdan*). Qur'an 5:5 says the same of men. "Taking secret lovers" describes exactly the private relationships between unmarried people that the narrowed definition would permit, and the verses exclude them.
- **Qur'an 17:32** says: *"Do not approach zina. It is an immorality and an evil way."* The verse is general, with no limitation to marriage.

The narrowed meaning does not fit these passages. It also has no support in early Arabic usage or in the understanding of any early generation. That does not settle every ethical question people may ask about sexual morality. It does show that the dispute can be decided by evidence, and that a reading has to account for **all** the places a word appears.

## Why this matters

The claim that texts "mean whatever the reader understands" is usually made selectively, about the texts one dislikes. Applied consistently, it would make every text meaningless, including the reader's own writing. Abdullah al-'Ujayri, in *Yanbu' al-Ghawaya al-Fikriyya*, discusses the danger of "emptying texts" of their meaning. The alternative is not rigid literalism. It is **disciplined reading**, attentive to language, context and the whole body of evidence. See also [the plural "We"](article.html?id=51), where the same method resolves a different question.
