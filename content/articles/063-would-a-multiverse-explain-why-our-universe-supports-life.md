Physicists have found that several features of our universe, such as the strength of gravity, the masses of particles and the energy of empty space, fall within ranges that permit complex chemistry and life. Small changes in some of them would, on current models, produce a universe without stars, atoms or life. This is called the **fine-tuning** problem: why do the constants fall where they do?

## The multiverse answer

One proposed answer is that ours is not the only universe. Suppose there is a vast collection of universes with different constants. Most would be lifeless. Observers could only ever arise in the rare ones that permit life. So of course we find ourselves in such a universe, because we could not find ourselves anywhere else.

This is a **selection effect**, and the reasoning is valid as far as it goes. A fisherman whose net has holes ten centimetres wide should not be surprised that every fish he catches is longer than ten centimetres.

## Questions that remain

A selection effect explains why we *observe* a life-permitting universe, **if** a suitable multiverse exists. Several questions follow:

- **Is there evidence for the other universes?** By most definitions they cannot be observed directly. Physicists debate whether multiverse models make testable predictions, and how such predictions could be checked.
- **What produces the universes?** Proposals usually rely on a mechanism, such as cosmic inflation combined with a "landscape" of possible physical states. That mechanism has its own laws and conditions. Why does *it* exist, and why is it of a kind that can produce a life-permitting universe at all?
- **How are probabilities assigned?** To say life-permitting universes are "rare but inevitable," the model needs a well-defined way to count universes and their properties. That is a serious open problem in cosmology.
- **Does it remove the question or move it?** If the generating mechanism is itself finely balanced, the question has been pushed back one level, not answered.

Richard Dawkins and the physicist Steven Weinberg discussed these points in a well-known conversation about the constants and the number of possible settings. It shows how open the question was, even among those who favour a naturalistic answer.

## A Muslim perspective: many worlds are not a threat

Muslims have no theological objection to the idea of other universes. The Qur'an opens by calling God *"Lord of the worlds"* (*rabb al-'alamin*, 1:2). In his commentary on that verse, Fakhr al-Din al-Razi argued that nothing prevents God from creating a thousand thousand worlds beyond this one, each larger than ours, and that the arguments some philosophers gave against this were weak. Ibn Kathir explains *al-'alamin* as all that exists other than God, in all its kinds.

So the question for a Muslim is not "Is there a multiverse?" That is for physics to investigate. The question is whether a multiverse, **if it exists**, would make a Creator unnecessary. It would not, for the same reasons that [laws of nature](article.html?id=61) do not. A collection of universes, and the mechanism that generates them, is still a dependent, law-governed reality whose existence needs explaining (see [who created God?](article.html?id=60)). The Qur'an's statement covers every world there is: *"He created everything and determined it with precise measure"* (25:2).

## Weighing it honestly

If a multiverse model gained strong observational support, it would explain some features of our universe, and that would be a real scientific achievement. Whether the multiverse itself and the principles behind it have a further explanation would still be an open philosophical question. Fine-tuning by itself does not prove God. It is one of several clues, alongside [origin, order and providence](article.html?id=58), that a wise Creator explains naturally.
