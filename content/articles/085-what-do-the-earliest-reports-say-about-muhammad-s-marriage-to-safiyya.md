Safiyya bint Huyayy ibn Akhtab was the daughter of the leader of Banu al-Nadir, a Jewish tribe exiled from Medina to Khaybar. When the Muslims took Khaybar in 628, her husband Kinana was killed and she was among the captives. Shortly afterwards she married the Prophet ﷺ. Critics often claim that the marriage was consummated on the very day her husband died. What do the earliest sources say?

## The timeline in the sources

**Freedom as the bridal gift.** Anas ibn Malik, who was present, reports that the Prophet freed Safiyya and made her freedom her *mahr*, her bridal gift (Sahih al-Bukhari 5086; Sahih Muslim 1365). In Islamic law the *mahr* belongs to the bride. Here the gift was her freedom itself, so she married as a free woman, not as a captive.

**The waiting period.** Anas also describes the journey back from Khaybar. The Prophet waited until Safiyya had become clear of her waiting period (*hallat*), and the marriage was celebrated at **Sadd al-Rawha'**, a stopping place on the road to Medina (Sahih al-Bukhari 2235). Islamic law requires a waiting period after a woman's previous marriage ends, which for a captive is at least one menstrual cycle. Its purpose is to establish that she is not pregnant. The marriage therefore did not take place on the day of the battle.

## Later life together

Several reports show how Safiyya lived as one of the Prophet's wives:

- She visited him in the mosque while he was in seclusion (*i'tikaf*) during Ramadan, and he walked her back home (Sahih al-Bukhari 2035). Safiyya herself narrated this, so it is her own memory of their relationship.
- When another wife called her "the daughter of a Jew," she came to the Prophet in tears. He told her: *"You are the daughter of a prophet, your uncle is a prophet, and you are married to a prophet. So how can she boast over you?"* referring to Aaron and Moses (Jami' al-Tirmidhi 3894).
- She remained devoted to him. Later biographical works report her grief at his final illness.

Ibn Sa'd's *Tabaqat* includes a report that the Prophet offered Safiyya a choice: freedom and return to her people, or freedom and marriage to him, and that she chose him. That report comes through the historian al-Waqidi, whom hadith critics regard as weak, so it carries less weight than the Sahih reports. It is consistent with them, but it does not establish the details on its own.

## Refusal was possible

Sahih al-Bukhari also preserves a case in which a different woman, the daughter of al-Jawn, refused the Prophet. When he approached her she said, *"I seek refuge in God from you."* He replied, *"You have sought refuge with the Great One. Return to your family,"* and sent her home with gifts (Sahih al-Bukhari 5254). The record therefore did preserve a woman's refusal, and it shows the Prophet accepting it.

## What the sources can and cannot establish

The reports answer the specific claim about timing. Marriage followed manumission, it came after the waiting period, and it took place days later on the road. They also give evidence of Safiyya's own memory of the relationship and of the Prophet's regard for her.

They do not directly record her inner decision, or what alternatives were realistically open to a woman whose husband and father had died in the conflict. A modern reader may still ask about the freedom of a choice made in those circumstances. The timeline and the question of consent are separate questions. Each should be judged on its own evidence.
