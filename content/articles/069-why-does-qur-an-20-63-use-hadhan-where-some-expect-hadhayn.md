In Qur’an 20:63, people call Moses and Aaron two magicians. In the familiar Ḥafṣ reading, the word for “these two” is *hādhāni*. Readers may expect *hādhayni* if they assume the preceding particle works like the familiar emphatic *inna*.

Classical grammar was codified after the Qur’an and draws on earlier Arabic, including poetry and regional speech. Ibn Aqil describes a usage in which the dual keeps its *-ān* ending across grammatical cases. Ibn Faris records similar variation. The same pattern appears in a report on voluntary prayers and in a verse of early Arabic poetry. Grammarians also offer other analyses, including taking the lighter particle *in* in the Ḥafṣ reading as one that leaves the case of the subject unchanged.

Modern readers can ask which reading they have, how its syntax is analyzed, and whether the form is attested elsewhere in early Arabic. That gives a stronger basis for judgment than applying one later classroom rule to every early usage.
