In Qur'an 20:63, Pharaoh's sorcerers say of Moses and Aaron: *"These two are surely magicians who want to drive you out of your land with their magic."* In the most widely recited reading today, that of Hafs from 'Asim, the Arabic is:

> ﴿إِنْ هَٰذَانِ لَسَاحِرَانِ﴾

Students of Arabic grammar learn that after the emphatic particle *inna*, a noun takes the accusative case, so the dual "these two" would be *hādhayni*, not *hādhāni*. Some critics present the verse as a grammatical error. There are several reasons it is not.

## 1. Grammar describes the language; it came later

Arabic grammar was systematised in the eighth and ninth centuries, a century or more after the Qur'an, by scholars who collected the speech of the Arabs: their poetry, proverbs and tribal usage. The Qur'an was itself one of their primary sources. A rule taught in a modern textbook summarises the **most common** pattern. It does not record every pattern that was actually in use.

## 2. A tribal usage: the dual keeps -ān in every case

Ibn 'Aqil, in his standard commentary on Ibn Malik's *Alfiyya*, records a dialect in which the dual keeps its *-ān* ending in all grammatical cases, nominative, accusative and genitive alike. Grammarians attribute it to tribes including Banu al-Harith ibn Ka'b and Kinana. Ibn Faris, in *al-Sahibi fi Fiqh al-Lugha*, records the same variation. A frequently quoted line of early poetry shows it:

> *inna abāhā wa-abā abāhā / qad balaghā fī al-majdi ghāyatāhā*
> "Her father and her father's father have reached the utmost peaks of glory."

Here *ghāyatāhā* keeps the *-ā* of the dual where standard grammar would expect *ghāyatayhā*.

The same pattern appears in a hadith. The Prophet ﷺ said: *"lā witrāni fī laylatin"*, "There are no two witr prayers in one night" (Sunan Abi Dawud 1439). After *lā* of negation, standard grammar would expect *witrayni*.

So the form in 20:63 is not an anomaly invented by the Qur'an. It is attested Arabic, used by real speakers.

## 3. In the Hafs reading, the particle is the lightened "in"

In the Hafs reading, the first word is not the heavy *inna* but the lightened *in* (*in mukhaffafa*). When *inna* is lightened in this way, Arabic generally lets it leave the case of what follows unchanged, and the sentence takes an emphatic *la-* on the predicate (*la-sāḥirāni*), exactly as here. On this analysis *hādhāni* is the normal nominative, and there is nothing to explain at all.

## 4. The readings record the variation

The Qur'an was transmitted orally in several canonical readings, all traced to the Prophet. In 20:63 they differ. Abu 'Amr reads *inna hādhayni*, the form standard grammar expects. Others read *inna hādhāni*, and Hafs reads *in hādhāni*. Al-Tabari discusses these readings and the grammarians' analyses in his commentary. The variation shows that early authorities were fully aware of the question and accepted the forms as authentic Arabic. They were not covering up a mistake.

A report attributed to 'A'isha, in which she is said to have called certain forms errors of the scribes, is often cited by critics. Classical scholars examined it and did not accept it as a reason to doubt the text. The readings were transmitted by large numbers of reciters, they correspond to known Arabic usage, and the report itself is open to question.

## How to evaluate a claim like this

When someone claims a grammatical error in the Qur'an, three questions help:

1. **Which reading is being discussed?** The answer may differ between readings.
2. **How do classical grammarians analyse the syntax?** They have usually discussed the verse in detail.
3. **Is the form attested elsewhere in early Arabic**, in poetry, hadith or tribal speech?

That gives a far stronger basis for judgment than applying one later classroom rule to every early usage. See also the [two forms of "Sabians"](article.html?id=70).
