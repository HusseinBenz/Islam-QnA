If the Quran is not from God, then someone wrote it, and the obvious candidate is the man who recited it. So it is worth asking honestly whether Muhammad ﷺ could have been its author.

## He was not a writer

He could neither read nor write, and his people knew it:

> *"You did not recite any book before this, nor did you write one with your right hand. Otherwise those who follow falsehood would have had cause for doubt."* — Quran 29:48

He lived among them for forty years without composing a line of poetry. Then, suddenly, came a recitation unlike anything in Arabic: neither poetry nor ordinary prose, and different even from his own everyday speech, which is preserved separately in the hadith.

## The challenge no one met

The Arabs of that age prized eloquence above almost everything. The Quran challenged them openly to produce even one chapter like it (2:23), and declared that they never would:

> *"Say: If mankind and jinn gathered to produce the like of this Quran, they could not produce the like of it, even if they helped one another."* — Quran 17:88

His enemies had every reason to answer. Meeting the challenge would have ended his mission without a single battle. Instead they chose boycott, torture and war, which is not what people do when a few lines of verse would settle the matter.

## A strange book for a man to invent

An author writes to serve himself. The Quran repeatedly does the opposite:

- It **rebukes him** for frowning at a blind man who interrupted him (80:1–10), and corrects him on other occasions (66:1, 9:43).
- When his wife Aisha was slandered, he waited in anguish for about a month with no answer. A forger would have produced a verse on the first day. Revelation came only when God willed (Sahih al-Bukhari 4750).
- When his infant son Ibrahim died, the sun was eclipsed, and people said it mourned for him. An impostor would have welcomed the story. He stood and said: "The sun and the moon are two signs of God. They are not eclipsed for the death or life of anyone" (Sahih al-Bukhari 1043).

## It invites you to prove it wrong

> *"Do they not reflect on the Quran? Had it been from other than Allah, they would have found in it much contradiction."* — Quran 4:82

It was revealed over twenty-three years, in peace and war, in answer to unplanned events, and it remains consistent. It also took risks no forger takes. When the Persians crushed the Byzantines, it announced that the Byzantines would win again "within a few years" (30:2–4), and they did. It declared that Abu Lahab, a living enemy, would die in disbelief (chapter 111). For years he could have discredited the Quran by simply pretending to accept Islam. He never did. Gary Miller, a mathematician, builds much of *The Amazing Qur'an* on this willingness to be tested.

## Liar, deluded, or truthful

There are only three possibilities. A liar seeks gain, but he refused wealth and kingship and died owning almost nothing. A deluded man does not build, in twenty-three years, a consistent law, a moral code and a civilisation. As the historian W. Montgomery Watt, who was not a Muslim, concluded:

> *"To suppose Muhammad an impostor raises more problems than it solves."* — W. Montgomery Watt, *Muhammad at Mecca*

That leaves the third option, the one the Quran states about itself. Muhammad Abdullah Draz works through this reasoning step by step in *The Qur'an: An Eternal Challenge*. The French physician Maurice Bucaille, after comparing the scriptures with modern knowledge, reported that he could find in the Quran no statement that contradicted established science.

The best test is still the one the Quran proposes: read it.
