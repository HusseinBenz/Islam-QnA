In 1887 Albert Michelson and Edward Morley set out to measure the earth's motion through the "luminiferous ether," the medium that the leading physics of the day assumed light needed in order to travel. Their extremely precise apparatus found almost none of the expected effect. In 1905 Einstein's special relativity explained the behaviour of light without any ether. Around the same time, research on atoms revealed the limits of classical physics.

Classical physics was not thrown away. Its equations still work superbly for bridges, orbits and engines. But measurements uncovered limits in a highly successful theory, and confident claims about the ether turned out to be wrong.

## Why the example matters

Science is our best method for studying nature, and it is self-correcting. That is its strength. It also means that **not every scientific claim has the same status**. Some are direct observations, some are well-confirmed theories, some are leading hypotheses, and some are philosophical interpretations attached to science. Hasan al-Asmari's critical study of modern scientific theories and Mustafa Mahmud's popular book on Einstein both discuss how theories of this kind are revised.

The same is true on the religious side. A clear verse is different from one scholar's interpretation of an ambiguous one. A mass-transmitted teaching is different from a weak report.

## The classical principle

Ibn Taymiyya argued in *Dar' Ta'arud al-'Aql wa-l-Naql* that what is **established with certainty** by reason or observation does not conflict with what is **authentically revealed and correctly understood**. When they seem to conflict, one of the two has been overstated: the "established" finding is not established, or the text is not authentic, or it has been misread. That is a theological position. Testing any specific claim of conflict still takes work on both sides.

## Three steps

1. **Name the claim exactly.** What did the study actually observe? What is the proposed explanation? Is this a direct finding or an extrapolation?
2. **Read the text in context.** How certain are its wording, its transmission and its interpretation? Did classical commentators read it differently from the critic? ([See the sun and the spring](article.html?id=71) and [the "flat" earth](article.html?id=72).)
3. **Compare the two.** Do they make incompatible claims **about the same thing, in the same respect**? Or does one describe appearance and the other mechanism, or one *how* and the other *why*?

If the evidence on either side is incomplete, it is honest to **leave the question open** ([see here](article.html?id=50)).

## Two opposite mistakes

**Forcing a conflict.** Reading a verse in the most literal way possible, ignoring Arabic usage and context, in order to set it against science. Critics do this, and so do some believers who then feel obliged to reject the science.

**Forcing a "miracle."** Reading the latest scientific theory into a verse that does not clearly say it, so as to claim the Qur'an predicted it. Some popular writers do this enthusiastically. The danger is obvious: if the theory changes, as the ether did, the "miracle" collapses, and the Qur'an appears to fall with it. Al-Shatibi already warned in *al-Muwafaqat* against claiming that the Qur'an contains every science, and against reading meanings into it that the Arabs who first heard it could not have understood.

The Qur'an is a book of guidance. It speaks about nature to point people to God, in language they can understand ([see here](article.html?id=58)). It does not need to be turned into a textbook to be true.

## The deeper conflict

Many apparent conflicts are really between Islam and **philosophical claims** made in the name of science: that only matter exists, that only experiments yield knowledge, that the universe explains itself. Those are not scientific findings, and they should be discussed as philosophy ([see here](article.html?id=121)). See also [does evolution rule out a creator?](article.html?id=62)
