A common objection runs: "The Prophet forbade writing hadith, so the hadith collections go against his own wishes." The objection relies on one report. Read it in full, and then read the others on the same subject.

## The report, in full

Abu Sa'id al-Khudri narrates that the Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Do not write anything from me except the Qur'an. Whoever has written anything from me other than the Qur'an, let him erase it. **And narrate from me; there is no harm in that.** Whoever lies about me deliberately, let him take his seat in the Fire."* (Sahih Muslim 3004)

Read only the first half and you hear a restriction. Read the whole and you find two instructions about **two different ways of preserving** his words. Writing is restricted, and oral narration is expressly commanded, with a warning against lying. The same hadith that is quoted against the Sunnah instructs people to transmit it.

## Other reports on writing

- **Abu Shah.** After the Prophet's sermon at the conquest of Mecca, a man from Yemen called Abu Shah asked for it to be written down for him. The Prophet said: *"Write it for Abu Shah"* (Sahih al-Bukhari 2434; Sahih Muslim 1355).
- **'Abd Allah ibn 'Amr.** He used to write down everything he heard from the Prophet. When some people told him to stop, the Prophet said: *"Write. By Him in whose hand is my soul, nothing comes from it but truth,"* pointing to his mouth (Sunan Abi Dawud 3646).
- **Abu Hurayra's testimony.** *"None of the Companions of the Prophet narrated more hadith than I, except 'Abd Allah ibn 'Amr, for he used to write and I did not"* (Sahih al-Bukhari 113).
- **The Prophet's own letters.** He dictated letters to rulers and written documents on alms and blood money, which were preserved.

## How scholars reconciled them

Ibn Hajar, in *Fath al-Bari*, and Ibn 'Abd al-Barr, in *Jami' Bayan al-'Ilm wa-Fadlih*, record several explanations:

1. **Abrogation.** The restriction came early, and permission came later, once the Qur'an was well established. The Abu Shah episode was near the end of the Prophet's life.
2. **Preventing confusion.** The restriction applied while the Qur'an was being revealed, so that nothing else would be written on the same sheets and mistaken for it.
3. **Specific individuals.** Permission was given to those who wrote accurately, such as 'Abd Allah ibn 'Amr, and caution applied to others.
4. **A question about the report's wording.** Ibn Hajar notes that al-Bukhari regarded the restrictive statement as the words of Abu Sa'id himself, not of the Prophet.

Al-Khatib al-Baghdadi's *Taqyid al-'Ilm* gathers all the early reports for and against writing, and shows that writing spread steadily among the Companions and their students.

## Why this does not settle the authority of the Sunnah

Notice what the objection does. It relies on **a transmitted hadith** to argue that transmitted hadith should not be relied on, while leaving out other reports transmitted in exactly the same way. If Sahih Muslim 3004 is reliable enough to quote, so are the reports permitting writing, and so is the second half of 3004 itself.

More fundamentally, the question of **when hadith began to be written** is a historical question about methods of preservation. Oral transmission was the primary method in that culture, and it was commanded in the very report under discussion. Whether the Sunnah has authority rests on the Qur'an's own commands ([see here](article.html?id=96)). See also [how reports were preserved before the major books](article.html?id=100).
