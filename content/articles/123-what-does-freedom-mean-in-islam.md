When two people say "freedom," they may mean quite different things. One may mean **being free from coercion**: no one forces me to believe or act. The other may mean **being free to choose well**, even when desire, habit or social pressure pull the other way. Islamic teaching speaks to both.

## Freedom from coercion in belief

> *"There is no compulsion in religion. The right course has become clear from the wrong."* (Quran 2:256)

> *"Had your Lord willed, all who are on earth would have believed. Would you then compel people until they become believers?"* (Quran 10:99)

> *"Say: The truth is from your Lord, so whoever wills, let him believe, and whoever wills, let him disbelieve."* (Quran 18:29)

Faith that is coerced is not faith. The commentators, including al-Tabari and Ibn Kathir, read these verses as forbidding forcing anyone to enter Islam. Non-Muslim communities under Muslim rule kept their religions under arrangements of protection and obligation, known as *dhimma*, in which they paid the *jizya* tax. How this relates to the classical ruling on leaving Islam is discussed [separately](article.html?id=107).

## Freedom from servitude

The second sense goes deeper. In Islamic teaching every person serves something. The question is what. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> *"Wretched is the slave of the dinar, the slave of the dirham, the slave of fine clothing. If he is given, he is pleased; if he is not given, he is displeased."* (Sahih al-Bukhari 2887)

A person can be outwardly free and inwardly ruled by money, possessions or appetite. The Qur'an describes *"the one who takes his own desire as his god"* (45:23). Worship of God alone, on this view, is the truest liberation, because it frees a person from servitude to everything less ([see here](article.html?id=64)).

When the Muslim envoy Rib'i ibn 'Amir was asked by the Persian commander Rustam why the Muslims had come, he is reported to have answered that God had sent them *"to bring people out from the worship of servants to the worship of the Lord of servants, from the narrowness of this world to its breadth, and from the injustice of religions to the justice of Islam."* Ibn Kathir records the exchange in *al-Bidaya wa-l-Nihaya*.

## Freedom within responsibility

Islamic teaching does not treat individual freedom as the final value that overrides everything else. It places freedom **within** responsibility to God and duties to others. Several passages show limits on conduct:

- **Moses and the golden calf.** Moses did not leave the calf as a matter of personal choice. He destroyed it and banished al-Samiri (20:97).
- **The duty to challenge wrongdoing.** The Qur'an criticises those who *"did not prevent one another from the wrong they did"* (5:79).
- **Public law.** Qur'an 24:2 sets a legal penalty for unlawful sexual relations. That shows that some conduct was treated as a public matter, not only a private one ([see here](article.html?id=106)).

These passages concern different subjects: idolatry within the community, public wrongdoing, and legal penalties. Working out the scope of freedom under Islamic law is therefore a question of **how these texts fit together** with "no compulsion" and the others. It is best answered by weighing the context and subject of each passage before drawing a general rule.

## Comparing Islamic and liberal accounts

Modern liberal thought often grounds freedom in individual autonomy, typically limited by a principle against harming others. Islamic thought grounds it in human dignity as God's creation and in accountability to Him. The two overlap in places, for example in rejecting coercion in belief, protecting life and property, and limiting arbitrary power. They differ in their foundations, and so sometimes in their conclusions ([see why people disagree about rules](article.html?id=47)).

A fair comparison examines both accounts **on their merits**, including the questions each finds hard: for liberalism, what grounds the dignity it protects; for Islamic law, how its historical rulings apply in modern states.
