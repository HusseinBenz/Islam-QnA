Three verses list the same groups: believers, Jews, Christians and Sabians. Two of them (2:62 and 5:69) promise that whoever among them truly believes in God and the Last Day and does good has nothing to fear. The third (22:17) adds the Magians and the polytheists and says that God will judge between them all on the Day of Resurrection. English translations usually render the word "the Sabians" every time. In Arabic, the ending changes:

> ﴿إِنَّ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَالَّذِينَ هَادُوا وَالنَّصَارَىٰ وَالصَّابِئِينَ﴾ (Quran 2:62): ***al-sābi'īn***

> ﴿إِنَّ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَالَّذِينَ هَادُوا وَالصَّابِئِينَ وَالنَّصَارَىٰ وَالْمَجُوسَ﴾ (Quran 22:17): ***al-sābi'īn***

> ﴿إِنَّ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَالَّذِينَ هَادُوا وَالصَّابِئُونَ وَالنَّصَارَىٰ﴾ (Quran 5:69): ***al-sābi'ūn***

In Arabic the final syllable shows what job a word does in the sentence. After *inna*, a noun and everything simply listed with it take the accusative (*-īn*). So why does 5:69 have the nominative *-ūn*?

## The grammarians' analysis

The most widely accepted analysis goes back to Sibawayh, the founder of Arabic grammar, and is recorded by al-Tabari, al-Qurtubi and Ibn 'Ashur. It holds that in 5:69 *al-sābi'ūn* is **not** part of the list governed by *inna*. It begins a separate clause, and its predicate is understood from the context. The meaning is:

> "Those who believe, and those who are Jews, and the Christians, whoever believes in God and the Last Day... *and the Sabians likewise*."

The phrase "and the Sabians likewise" has been placed earlier in the sentence than its natural position, and its nominative ending signals that it stands apart from the list.

Ibn 'Ashur notes that the Arabs used this construction, and cites a well-known line by the pre-Islamic poet Dabi' ibn al-Harith al-Burjumi:

> *fa-man yaku amsā bi-l-madīnati raḥluhu / fa-innī wa-qayyārun bihā la-gharību*
> "Whoever has made his home in the city, I and Qayyar (my camel) are surely strangers there."

After *innī* ("surely I"), the expected form would be accusative, *qayyāran*. The poet uses the nominative *qayyārun*, because "and Qayyar" is a separate clause ("and Qayyar is likewise a stranger") placed early. It is the same structure as 5:69.

## Why the emphasis?

Commentators also asked what the construction *does*. Ibn 'Ashur, following earlier scholars such as al-Zamakhshari, explains that setting the Sabians apart draws attention to them. They were regarded as the group furthest from the guidance of the earlier scriptures. The effect is: *even the Sabians*, if they truly believe and do good, will be accepted. The change of ending is a rhetorical device, not an accident. A careful listener in seventh-century Arabia would hear the shift and register the emphasis.

The variation between the three verses also shows that the text was not "corrected" to make it uniform. The forms were preserved exactly as they were recited.

## The general lesson

English usually cannot show the grammatical role of a word through its ending, so this kind of difference disappears in translation. When a verse seems to break a rule:

1. Read it in Arabic, with its full sentence structure.
2. Check how the classical grammarians and commentators analysed it.
3. Ask whether the construction is attested in early Arabic poetry and speech.
