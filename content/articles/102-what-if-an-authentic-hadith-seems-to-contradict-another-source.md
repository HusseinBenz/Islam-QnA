Sometimes a sound hadith seems to conflict with a verse of the Qur'an, with another hadith, or with reason, observation or science. That can be unsettling. It helps to know that this is an old question, and that the earliest Muslims asked it openly.

## The Companions asked too

**Hafsa.** The Prophet ﷺ said that none of those who pledged allegiance under the tree at Hudaybiya would enter the Fire. His wife Hafsa objected by quoting the Qur'an: *"There is none of you but will pass over it"* (19:71). The Prophet replied by reciting the next verse: *"Then We will save those who feared God"* (19:72). Passing over is not the same as entering to be punished (Sahih Muslim 2496).

**Aisha.** The Prophet said, *"Whoever is called to account will be punished."* Aisha asked: does God not say, *"He will be judged with an easy reckoning"* (84:8)? He explained that the easy reckoning is only the **presentation** of deeds. Whoever is **interrogated** about his deeds is ruined (Sahih al-Bukhari 103).

In both cases the apparent conflict came from treating two different things as one. Close reading resolved it.

## A method in steps

Classical legal theorists treated conflicting evidence (*ta'arud al-adilla*) as a problem in its own right. Their method is broadly as follows:

1. **Establish the text.** Is the hadith really sound? What is its best-attested wording? Do other versions clarify it? ([See here](article.html?id=101).)
2. **Read the context.** Who was addressed, when, and about what?
3. **Ask what each source actually claims.** Two statements may concern different people, different circumstances, or different aspects of one thing. Is one general and the other specific?
4. **Reconcile (*jam'*).** If both can be true together, that is the preferred answer.
5. **Determine sequence (*naskh*).** If they cannot be reconciled and one clearly came later, the later may supersede the earlier in law.
6. **Weigh (*tarjih*).** If neither works, give preference to the better-established evidence.

## An example involving observation

The Prophet ﷺ said: *"There is no contagion (la 'adwa)... and flee from the leper as you would flee from a lion"* (Sahih al-Bukhari 5707). He also said that a sick animal should not be brought among healthy ones (Sahih al-Bukhari 5771). Was he denying contagion while commanding precautions against it?

Ibn Qutayba, in *Ta'wil Mukhtalif al-Hadith*, and later scholars explained that the denial targets the pre-Islamic belief that disease spreads **by its own independent power**, apart from God's decree. The precautions recognise transmission as a real cause **within** God's decree. Observation and the text, read together, agree. The same hadith also contains both statements, which shows that they were not felt to be in contradiction.

## The literature

Muslim scholars wrote whole books on this:

- **Al-Shafi'i**, *Ikhtilaf al-Hadith*, the first known work on reconciling reports.
- **Ibn Qutayba**, *Ta'wil Mukhtalif al-Hadith*, a ninth-century reply to critics who claimed hadith contradict reason or one another.
- **Al-Tahawi**, *Sharh Mushkil al-Athar*, a vast study of difficult reports.
- Modern studies such as 'Isa al-Na'mi's *Daf' Da'wa al-Mu'arid al-'Aqli*, on reports in the two Sahih collections said to conflict with reason, Lutfi al-Zughayr's *al-Ta'arud fi al-Hadith al-Nabawi*, and Sulayman al-Dubaykhi's *Ahadith al-'Aqida al-Mutawahham Ishkaluha fi al-Sahihayn*.

## When the other source is science

When a report seems to conflict with a scientific claim, the same steps apply to **both** sides. What does the hadith actually say, in its best wording and context? What does the scientific evidence actually establish, and with what confidence? Is the scientific claim an observation or an interpretation of observations? Often the conflict lies between one **reading** of the text and one **interpretation** of the evidence. See [claims that science and Islam conflict](article.html?id=122).

If a question remains open after honest work, it is reasonable to say so and keep looking ([see here](article.html?id=50)).
