A thermometer can measure temperature, but it cannot tell you whether an argument is valid. Logic can tell you whether a conclusion follows from its premises, but it cannot tell you what happened in Mecca in 610. A reliable witness can tell you that, though a witness cannot measure temperature. Different questions need different tools.

## The three sources

A short creed taught for centuries in Sunni schools, *al-'Aqa'id al-Nasafiyya*, opens with this principle: the means of knowledge for people are three. They are **sound senses, truthful report and reason**. The Qur'an points to the same tools: *"God brought you out of your mothers' wombs knowing nothing, and gave you hearing, sight and hearts, that you might be grateful"* (16:78).

- **The senses** give us direct contact with the world: what we see, hear and measure. Science systematically extends them.
- **Reason** draws conclusions from what we know. It tells us that something which begins must have a cause, that a contradiction cannot be true, and what follows from a set of premises.
- **Truthful report** (*khabar sadiq*) gives knowledge of what we did not witness. Most of what anyone knows about history, geography, or even their own date of birth comes this way. Revelation, for Muslims, is the most important truthful report, and it is accepted because reason and evidence show that the messenger was truthful.

The tools work together. You reason about what an observation shows, and you use independent reports to confirm each other.

## When a debate is really about evidence

Some disagreements about God are not really about God. They are about which of these sources counts. If someone accepts **only** what can be shown by experiment, then rational arguments and historical testimony will not register at all.

It is worth asking two questions about that rule. First, **can the rule itself be shown by experiment?** No laboratory test establishes "only experimentally tested claims are knowledge." The rule is a philosophical claim, so by its own standard it should be rejected. Second, **what else would the rule rule out?** All of history, most of law, and much of everyday life depend on testimony and reasoning, not experiment. Ibn Taymiyya made a related point at length in *Dar' Ta'arud al-'Aql wa-l-Naql*. Sound reason and authentic revelation are both sources of knowledge, and neither should be set up as the enemy of the other. See [can science answer every question?](article.html?id=121)

## Revelation and the scholars

In Sunni thought, the Qur'an and the authentic Sunnah are God's guidance. Two kinds of work still have to be done. One is **authentication**: is this report really from the Prophet? The other is **interpretation**: what does it mean, and how does it apply? That is why scholarship matters. It also means the standing of a scholar's view depends on its evidence.

Sunni legal theory therefore distinguishes levels of authority:

- the Qur'an and the established Sunnah;
- a verified **consensus** (*ijma'*) of the scholars, which al-Ghazali's *al-Mustasfa* and other works of legal theory treat as binding (see [why consensus carries weight](article.html?id=103));
- the reasoning (*ijtihad*) of an individual jurist, which is respected but can be mistaken.

A saying widely attributed to Malik ibn Anas, and recorded by Ibn 'Abd al-Barr, puts this plainly: *"Everyone's word may be accepted or rejected, except the occupant of this grave,"* meaning the Prophet ﷺ. The Qur'an gives the principle itself: *"If you disagree over anything, refer it to God and the Messenger"* (4:59).

## Trust is earned by evidence

None of this asks for blind trust. The Qur'an invites people to examine its claims: *"Do they not reflect upon the Qur'an? Had it been from other than God, they would have found in it much contradiction"* (4:82). It is reasonable to ask *why* a source deserves trust before following its guidance. That is what the questions on [the Qur'an's origin](article.html?id=16) and [the case for prophethood](article.html?id=19) are for.
