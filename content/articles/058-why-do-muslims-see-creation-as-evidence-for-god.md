The Qur'an calls the features of the natural world *ayat*, the same word it uses for its own verses. Both are "signs" that point beyond themselves:

> *"We will show them Our signs in the horizons and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that it is the truth."* (Quran 41:53)

> *"And on the earth are signs for those with certainty, and within yourselves. Do you not see?"* (Quran 51:20–21)

Muslim thinkers drew three main arguments from these signs.

## 1. Origin: why does anything that began exist?

When something begins to exist, we look for a cause. We do this so naturally that we would find it absurd to be told a building, or a person, simply appeared from nothing. The Qur'an applies this to human beings:

> *"Or were they created by nothing, or were they the creators of themselves? Or did they create the heavens and the earth? Rather, they are not certain."* (Quran 52:35–36)

> *"Does man not remember that We created him before, when he was nothing?"* (Quran 19:67)

Al-Ghazali put the same reasoning into a short syllogism in *al-Iqtisad fi al-I'tiqad*. Everything that begins has a cause. The world began. So the world has a cause. Since a chain of caused causes cannot explain itself, the chain must rest on a cause that did not begin.

Modern cosmology traces the expansion of the universe back to a hot, dense early state. Scientists debate what, if anything, preceded the earliest period that physics can describe. Those debates are worth following, but they are distinct from the philosophical question: does a universe that is dependent and changing need an ultimate cause that is neither? See [who created God?](article.html?id=60) and [do the laws of nature make a creator unnecessary?](article.html?id=61)

## 2. Order: why is the world intelligible?

Physical and biological systems show regular patterns that can be described mathematically and studied by science. Describing *how* those processes work is the task of science. A further question remains: **why is there a world of stable, intelligible order at all, and minds able to understand it?** The Qur'an invites the reader to look for flaws and find none:

> *"You do not see in the creation of the Most Merciful any inconsistency. So return your vision: do you see any breaks? Then return your vision twice again."* (Quran 67:3–4)

For Muslims, the intelligibility and complexity of the world point to wisdom. That conclusion holds even when we can describe the processes involved, just as understanding how a printing press works does not explain who wrote the book.

## 3. Providence: why does the world suit us so well?

The third argument looks at how living things and their environment fit together. The Qur'an repeatedly asks people to notice it:

> *"Let man look at his food: how We poured down water in abundance, then split the earth in clefts, and caused grain to grow in it, and grapes and herbs, and olives and palms..."* (Quran 80:24–29)

Other passages describe the pure milk drawn from cattle (16:66), the sea made navigable (16:14), the alternation of night and day (28:71–73), and humanity's ability to learn and use what it finds (2:31; 96:4–5). The Andalusian philosopher Ibn Rushd called this the **argument from providence** (*dalil al-'inaya*), and paired it with the **argument from invention** (*dalil al-ikhtira'*), which corresponds to the argument from origin above. He regarded both as the Qur'an's own method of proof.

## Weighing the arguments

None of these arguments is a laboratory experiment, and they are not offered as one. They are arguments to the **best explanation**. Given that the universe began, is ordered and suits life, which account of it makes the most sense? A reader can value the observations and still ask which explanation is strongest. The Qur'an asks for exactly that kind of reflection. Ibn Taymiyya stressed in his fatwas that the knowledge of God gained this way supports and awakens the knowledge already present in the human [fitrah](article.html?id=57).
