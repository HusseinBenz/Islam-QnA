Suppose someone proposes a new reading of a verse. If the reading adds a detail that earlier scholars simply did not discuss, it may be a genuine contribution. It is a different matter if the reading requires that **every** scholar from the Companions onward misunderstood a clear text. How strong would the evidence need to be for a claim like that? That question is what consensus (*ijma'*) addresses.

## What consensus is

In classical Sunni legal theory, *ijma'* is **the agreement of the qualified scholars (mujtahids) of the community, in a given generation, on a religious ruling.** Works of legal theory such as al-Juwayni's *al-Burhan* and al-Ghazali's *al-Mustasfa* treat a consensus, once established, as a binding proof.

Jurists understand that a consensus always has a **foundation** in the Qur'an or Sunnah, whether the link is direct or reached by inference. Ibn Taymiyya stressed that there is no true consensus without a textual basis, even if not every scholar who agreed cited it. Consensus does not create new religion. It confirms the meaning of what was revealed.

## The theological argument

Several verses ground the argument:

> *"And thus We have made you a middle community, that you may be witnesses over the people."* (Quran 2:143)

> *"You are the best community brought forth for mankind. You enjoin what is right and forbid what is wrong."* (Quran 3:110)

> *"Whoever opposes the Messenger after guidance has become clear to him and follows other than the way of the believers, We will turn him to what he has turned to and drive him into Hell."* (Quran 4:115)

Al-Shafi'i is reported to have taken 4:115 as the Qur'anic proof of consensus. To depart from the way the believers as a whole have agreed upon is linked with opposing the Messenger. Commentators including Ibn Kathir explain the verse in this light.

A well-known hadith adds: *"My community will not agree upon error"* (Sunan Ibn Majah 3950, and other routes). Its individual chains have weaknesses, and hadith critics discussed them. Many scholars held that its many routes, together with related reports, strengthen its meaning.

The argument does not claim any individual scholar is infallible. It claims that **God would not let the whole community of scholars agree on a falsehood in religion**. It is a promise about the community, not about any person in it.

## The historical argument

A second line of reasoning is historical. The Companions and the first generations:

- spoke the Qur'an's Arabic as their native language;
- knew the circumstances in which verses were revealed;
- watched the Prophet apply the teachings;
- transmitted the practice of worship directly.

Their shared understanding of a clear text is therefore strong evidence of its meaning. When a new interpretation claims that **all** early readers overlooked something obvious, the burden of proof on that interpretation is very heavy. This is why jurists treat debates about well-known rulings, such as the [penalty for adultery](article.html?id=106) or [apostasy](article.html?id=107), partly as debates about how established sources have been read.

## The caution

The authority of consensus does not make every **claim** of consensus true. Some writers asserted consensus too easily, when they simply knew of no disagreement. Any appeal to consensus therefore depends on showing **who agreed, on what question, and how securely we know it**. Some consensus is certain, for example that the daily prayers are five. Other claims are disputed. Imam Ahmad ibn Hanbal's famous warning on this point is discussed [in a separate answer](article.html?id=104).
