An interpretation might be new in the sense that it adds a detail earlier scholars did not address. The case is different when it requires overturning an agreement that can be traced through the tradition. The book asks what evidence could justify a claim that strong. The author applies this to disputes over stoning and the apostasy penalty, where he considers the disagreement to be about how well-known sources were read.

**The theological argument.** It points to the Quran's description of the community as witnesses and to its praise of a community that enjoins good. In classical Sunni legal theory, *ijmāʿ* is the agreement of qualified scholars on a religious question. Jurists understand that agreement to have a foundation in the Quran or Sunnah, whether the connection is direct or inferred.

**The historical argument.** The earliest Muslims were closer to the language, circumstances and examples the texts address. Muhammad's Companions could also observe his teaching. Those advantages matter when a new interpretation claims all early readers overlooked a clear meaning.

Any appeal to consensus still depends on showing who agreed, on what question, and how securely we know it.
