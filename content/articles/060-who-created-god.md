"If everything needs a creator, who created God?" It is one of the most common questions about faith, asked by children and philosophers alike. It deserves a real answer, and it has one.

## Who God is, in Islamic belief

The Qur'an describes God as eternal, without beginning:

> *"He is the First and the Last, the Manifest and the Hidden, and He has knowledge of all things."* (Quran 57:3)

> *"Say: He is God, One. God, the Eternal Refuge. He neither begets nor is born, nor is there to Him any equivalent."* (Quran 112:1–4)

The Prophet ﷺ explained the first of these names in a prayer before sleep: *"O God, You are the First, and there is nothing before You. You are the Last, and there is nothing after You"* (Sahih Muslim 2713).

So the question "Who created God?" asks who brought into being one who, by definition, was never brought into being. It is like asking who the bachelor's wife is. The question assumes the very thing that is being denied.

## Step one: what begins has a cause

The argument for God does **not** say "everything that exists has a cause." If it did, the question "Who created God?" would be fair. It says something narrower:

> **Whatever begins to exist has a cause.**

That is the premise al-Ghazali sets out in *al-Iqtisad fi al-I'tiqad*, and the Qur'an appeals to it: *"Or were they created by nothing, or were they the creators of themselves?"* (52:35). We apply it constantly. Nobody believes a house, a person or a planet popped into existence uncaused. The universe began, so it too needs a cause. A being who **did not** begin is not covered by the premise.

## Step two: a chain of dependent causes needs a foundation

Suppose someone says: "Fine, but maybe the cause of the universe had its own cause, and that one had another, and so on forever."

Ibn Taymiyya discusses this at length in *Dar' Ta'arud al-'Aql wa-l-Naql*. Consider a chain in which **every** member depends on something before it for its existence. Adding more members, even infinitely many, does not change the fact that each one is dependent. A train of carriages, each pulled by the one in front, does not move unless there is an engine. Making the train longer, even endlessly long, does not supply the engine. An infinite chain of borrowers, each borrowing from the one before, never produces the money.

So whatever ultimately explains the existence of dependent things must itself be **not dependent**: it exists by itself, without beginning, and without needing a cause. This is what Muslims mean by God. Whether the evidence points to this foundation being God, with knowledge, will and power, is the next question. The arguments from [order and providence](article.html?id=58) address it.

## When the question becomes a loop

The Prophet ﷺ knew this question could become an intrusive loop rather than an inquiry:

> *"Satan comes to one of you and says, 'Who created this? Who created that?' until he says, 'Who created your Lord?' When he reaches that, let him seek refuge in God and stop."* (Sahih al-Bukhari 3276)

Ibn Taymiyya comments in *Dar'* that the instruction to "stop" fits the reasoning above. A chain of "who made that?" questions has to end in something that was not made. Once it reaches that point, asking the same question again does not reflect a gap in the answer. It reflects a misunderstanding of it. Stopping is the rational response. If the question keeps returning as a source of distress rather than curiosity, see [questions and intrusive thoughts](article.html?id=53).
