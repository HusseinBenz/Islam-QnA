Sometimes you search and search and find nothing that addresses your question directly. That can feel alarming, as if the question has no answer. Usually it means one of three things: the question needs to be phrased more precisely, it needs a specialist, or it has been discussed in older sources under a different name.

## The Qur'an's instruction

> *"So ask the people of knowledge, if you do not know."* (Quran 16:43)

The verse does not say "stop asking." It says to ask the right people. The Companion 'Abd Allah ibn Mas'ud added a principle every honest inquirer should know: *"It is part of knowledge that, when a person does not know something, he says: God knows best"* (Sahih al-Bukhari, Book of Commentary, Surat al-Dukhan). Saying "I don't know" is part of knowing.

## Older sources often answered "new" questions

Many questions that seem modern were raised and discussed centuries ago. Three examples:

**"If God knows what I will do, how can He judge me for it?"** Classical theologians discussed this at length. God's knowledge is not what compels a person's choice. It is knowledge of the choice the person will freely make. The Qur'an depicts Iblis choosing to refuse God's command out of pride, and then deciding to tempt humanity (7:11–18; 18:50). It also affirms both human will and God's will: *"So whoever wills may take a way to his Lord. And you do not will unless God wills"* (76:29–30). See [decree and responsibility](article.html?id=67).

**"Could the magic reported against the Prophet have affected the revelation?"** Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim report that the Prophet ﷺ was once affected by sorcery, so that he imagined he had done something he had not done (Sahih al-Bukhari 3175, 5763; Sahih Muslim 2189). Al-Nawawi, in his commentary on Muslim, records the explanation of Qadi 'Iyad and al-Mazari. The effect concerned his body and private worldly matters, such as his dealings with his family. The delivery of revelation is protected by God, as the evidence for his truthfulness establishes, and the reports themselves say nothing about revelation.

**"Why does one hadith say the Dajjal is blind in the right eye, and another the left?"** (Sahih Muslim 169; Sahih Muslim 2934). Qadi 'Iyad, quoted by Ibn Hajar in *Fath al-Bari*, explained that the Arabic word *a'war* can describe different kinds of impairment. One eye may be blind and effaced while the other is defective and protruding, so both reports can be true.

The point is not that every question has been settled. It is that the tradition has a long habit of facing difficult texts openly. A specialist will often know where to look.

## How to ask well

1. **Write the question down precisely.** "Islam doesn't make sense about fate" cannot be answered. "How can God's prior knowledge coexist with my free choice?" can.
2. **Choose the right person.** Ask someone who will hear the whole question and can show where their answer comes from. A specialist in hadith for a hadith question, a jurist for a legal one.
3. **Accept different kinds of answer.** A good answer may be a reading list, a conversation, an explanation of a scholarly disagreement, or an honest "this is still debated."
4. **Use reference works.** Encyclopedias of responses, such as the Arabic *Bayan al-Islam*, gather many recurring questions in one place.

## Leaving a question open

The Qur'an describes people firmly grounded in knowledge as saying of difficult verses, *"We believe in it; all of it is from our Lord"* (3:7). That is not a refusal to think. It recognises that what you have strong reason to believe is not overturned by one point you cannot yet explain.

Keep a note of what is established and where you would like more evidence. Come back to it. Many people find, years later, that a question which once troubled them now has a clear answer, or no longer seems important.

**For parents and teachers:** listen to the whole question before explaining, and make it safe to ask again. A child who is told "don't ask that" will ask someone else.
