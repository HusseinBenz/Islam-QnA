The Quran asks whether the one who created the heavens and earth could bring people back to life. The argument begins with a premise the original audience could recognize. It also asks whether people came into being without a cause or created themselves. These passages invite the reader to compare explanations.

A man once questioned his child's parentage because of the child's skin color. Muhammad asked him about variation among his own camels, which led him to consider inherited traits. In another report, Muhammad asks which tree resembles a believer and gives the date palm as his answer. When he asks who is truly bankrupt, he describes a person who has acts of worship to their credit but harmed others, and whose good deeds are transferred to those they wronged. A modern example is how physicists infer the presence of particles from detector signals. The example shows why an inference can be rational without direct sight, although the kind of evidence varies between physics and theology.

A second method is to test an argument's internal consistency and consequences. If a person rejects hadith as evidence, ask what role a hadith plays when they cite it. If someone rejects an afterlife, ask what their view implies about harms that are never redressed in this life. Secular accounts can still recognize wrong and seek justice here. In Islam, resurrection also allows for ultimate accountability. A third method is to examine what reason can establish and when knowing the unseen depends on reliable revelation.
