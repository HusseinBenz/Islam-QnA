question: How does Sabighat answer the claim that jihad endorses indiscriminate violence?
summary: The book asks readers to judge armed groups by the relevant texts and historical context. Those texts contain protections for women and children, condemn treachery and mutilation, praise feeding captives, and provide for safe passage.

A group's religious label tells you how it describes itself. To assess what its actions have to do with Islamic teaching, the book asks you to compare those actions with specific sources. It applies the same standard across beliefs, using crimes by governments that espoused atheism as an example of why guilt belongs to the responsible actors and cannot be imputed to everyone with the same worldview.

**What limits do the sources give?** A hadith prohibits killing women and children in war. Other reports forbid treachery and mutilation. The Quran commends feeding a captive and directs that a person from the opposing side who seeks protection to hear the message be granted asylum for that purpose and escorted to safety. Muhammad's rebuke of Usama ibn Zayd concerns a man who professed faith during combat.

**How does the book explain the aims?** Al-Sayyid frames historical fighting in terms of conveying and defending the faith, seeking people's salvation, and removing barriers to religious choice. Classical jurists also discussed *jizya*, a public levy under which certain non-Muslim communities could retain their religion. These are claims about specific texts and institutions. Accounts of particular wars still need evidence of what actors did and why.
