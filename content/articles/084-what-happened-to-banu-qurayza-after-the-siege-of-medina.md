This is one of the most serious episodes critics raise about the Prophet's life, and it should be approached with care: first by establishing what the sources say, then by asking how to evaluate it.

## The setting

When the Prophet ﷺ came to Medina, its communities, including the Jewish tribes, entered into a written agreement of mutual defence. Ibn Ishaq's biography, edited by Ibn Hisham, preserves its text, often called the Constitution of Medina. Two other Jewish tribes, Banu Qaynuqa' and Banu al-Nadir, had already been expelled after earlier conflicts.

In 627 a coalition of Quraysh and allied tribes, some ten thousand strong, besieged Medina. The Muslims dug a trench and held the city. The Qur'an describes the fear of that time:

> *"When they came at you from above you and from below you, and when eyes shifted and hearts reached the throats, and you assumed about God assumptions. There the believers were tested and shaken with a severe shaking."* (Quran 33:10–11)

## The breach

According to Ibn Ishaq, the exiled leader of Banu al-Nadir, Huyayy ibn Akhtab, persuaded Ka'b ibn Asad, the leader of Banu Qurayza, to break the agreement and join the besiegers. Banu Qurayza lived inside the city's defensive perimeter, behind the Muslim lines. The Prophet sent envoys, including Sa'd ibn Mu'adh, to confirm the report, and they returned with confirmation of the breach. For the Muslims this meant an armed group allied with the enemy was at their backs, where the women and children were sheltering.

## The siege and the judgment

After the coalition withdrew, the Muslims besieged Banu Qurayza. Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim report that they surrendered on condition that they accept the judgment of Sa'd ibn Mu'adh, the leader of the Aws, who had long been their allies (Sahih al-Bukhari 3043, 4121; Sahih Muslim 1768). Sa'd ruled that **the fighting men be executed and the women and children taken captive.** The Prophet ﷺ said, *"You have judged according to the judgment of God."*

The Qur'an refers to the outcome:

> *"And He brought down those who supported them among the People of the Book from their fortresses and cast terror into their hearts. A party you killed, and a party you took captive."* (Quran 33:26)

## Who counted as a fighter

A report attributed to 'Atiyya al-Qurazi, one of the captives, says that those who had reached puberty were treated as fighters, and he was spared because he had not (Sunan Abi Dawud 4404). This matters for modern readers. Puberty and modern age categories can diverge, so "fighting men" in the sources may include young men who would not be considered adults today. The sources also record that some members of the tribe who had accepted Islam or had refused to join the breach were spared.

## The numbers

The hadith collections do not give a number. Ibn Ishaq gives 600 to 700, and some later accounts give higher figures. Some modern historians, including Muslim scholars, have questioned the larger figures and parts of the later narrative, noting that Ibn Ishaq drew partly on reports from descendants of the tribe. The core of the account, the treaty breach, the surrender to Sa'd's judgment and the ruling, is in the most reliable collections. The scale remains uncertain.

## How Muslim scholars explain it

Classical scholars framed the episode as a case of **treason in wartime by a group bound by treaty**, under siege conditions that threatened the community's survival. They noted that the tribe chose its own arbiter and the judgment was his. Some writers have also observed that Sa'd's ruling corresponds closely to the law of war in the Hebrew Bible for a besieged city that refuses terms (Deuteronomy 20:12–14), the law the tribe itself recognised.

Ibn Kathir, commenting on 33:26, presents the outcome as the consequence of their breach at the moment of greatest danger. The episode concerned **one group and one specific act**. It was not treated as a general rule about Jews. Jewish communities continued to live under Muslim rule in the following centuries.

## What remains for the reader

The sources explain the context, the legal reasoning and the conduct of the parties. They do not remove the severity of collective execution and captivity, and a reader may still need to evaluate it morally. Recognising that is part of reading history honestly.
