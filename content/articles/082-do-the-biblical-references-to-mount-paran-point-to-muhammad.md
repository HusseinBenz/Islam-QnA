Several passages in the Hebrew Bible mention Paran, and Muslim writers since the classical period have argued that they point to Arabia and to Muhammad. The argument joins several separate strands, and it is clearest to examine each one in turn.

## The passages

**Deuteronomy 33:2**, the opening of Moses' final blessing:

> *"The LORD came from Sinai and dawned on them from Seir. He shone forth from Mount Paran and came with myriads of holy ones; at his right hand a fiery law for them."*

**Habakkuk 3:3**:

> *"God came from Teman, the Holy One from Mount Paran. His splendour covers the heavens, and his praise fills the earth."*

**Genesis 21:21**, about Ishmael after he and Hagar were sent away:

> *"He lived in the wilderness of Paran."*

## The Muslim reading

Ibn Taymiyya in *al-Jawab al-Sahih*, Rahmatullah Kairanawi in *Izhar al-Haqq*, and in modern times Munqidh al-Saqqar in *Hal Bashsharat al-Kutub bi-Muhammad?*, build the argument in steps:

1. **Three places, three revelations.** Sinai is where the law was given to Moses. Seir is linked to the land where Jesus' message arose. Paran is linked to Arabia, where the Qur'an was revealed. Ibn Taymiyya compares this with the opening oath of Surat al-Tin: *"By the fig and the olive, and Mount Sinai, and this secure city"* (95:1–3), which many commentators read as alluding to the lands of Jesus, Moses and Muhammad.
2. **Paran and Ishmael.** Genesis places Ishmael in the wilderness of Paran. Islamic tradition places Ishmael in Mecca, where he and Abraham built the Ka'ba (Quran 2:127). So Paran, on this reading, is the Hejaz.
3. **"Myriads of holy ones."** When Muhammad entered Mecca in 630, his army numbered about ten thousand (Sahih al-Bukhari 4276). Some older translations of Deuteronomy 33:2 read "ten thousands of saints."
4. **"A fiery law."** The Qur'an brought a complete law.
5. **"His praise fills the earth."** In Habakkuk the word for "praise" is related in meaning to the root of *Muhammad* and *Ahmad*, "the praised."

## Evaluating each step

Each step can be assessed on its own:

- **Is Paran in Arabia?** Elsewhere the Bible places the wilderness of Paran near Sinai and Kadesh, south of Canaan (Numbers 10:12; 13:3, 26). Most biblical geographers locate it in the Sinai peninsula or the Negev. Muslim writers respond that a regional name could cover a wide area, and that Ishmael's descendants are associated with northern Arabia in Genesis 25. That is possible, but the texts do not settle it.
- **Is Seir connected with Jesus?** Mount Seir is usually Edom, south-east of the Dead Sea. Al-Saqqar argues that the name also appears near Judah (Joshua 15:10), and so could evoke the region of Jesus' ministry. A name recurring in another place shows the reading is *possible*. It does not show it is *intended*.
- **The past tense.** Deuteronomy says God *came* and *shone forth*. Muslim writers note that prophetic language often uses the past tense for certain future events. That is true of Hebrew prophecy, but it does not by itself show that this passage is a prophecy.
- **The holy ones.** Many modern translations read "holy ones" as angels or heavenly beings accompanying God, as in other biblical theophanies.

A common Jewish and Christian reading takes both passages as **poetic descriptions of God's appearance to Israel**, coming from the southern mountains where the Exodus took place, with Paran in the region south of Israel.

## How strong is the argument?

The Paran argument is internally coherent and has a long history among Muslim scholars. Its strength depends on accepting several identifications that the texts do not establish by themselves. Like the [Baca–Bakka link](article.html?id=81), it is best treated as a possible reading to be weighed against the alternatives, not as a decisive proof. The case for Muhammad's prophethood does not rest on it ([see here](article.html?id=19)).
