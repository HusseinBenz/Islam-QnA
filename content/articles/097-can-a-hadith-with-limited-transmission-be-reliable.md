You may be completely sure that a relative was born or has died, not because thousands of people told you, but because a few trusted people did and the details agree. The **number** of people who pass on news is only part of what makes it reliable. Who they are, how they know, and whether the details fit together matter just as much.

## What *ahad* means

In hadith terminology, reports are divided into two broad kinds:

- ***Mutawatir*** (mass-transmitted): reported by so many independent people at every stage that agreement on a falsehood is inconceivable.
- ***Ahad*** (literally "singular"): every report below that level. An *ahad* report may still have several transmitters at each stage. It is simply not mass-transmitted.

Most individual hadith are *ahad*. Some modern critics argue that because they are not mass-transmitted, they give only "conjecture" and cannot be relied on in religion.

## The Prophet relied on individuals

The Prophet ﷺ regularly sent **single envoys** to teach and to carry binding instructions. He sent Mu'adh ibn Jabal to Yemen and told him to teach the people the testimony of faith, the prayer and the alms (Sahih al-Bukhari 1395). He sent individual letters to rulers. Communities were expected to act on what one trustworthy messenger brought.

The Companions did the same. When the direction of prayer was changed from Jerusalem to Mecca, a single man came to the people of Quba' while they were praying the dawn prayer and told them. **They turned around in the middle of the prayer** (Sahih al-Bukhari 403).

## The Qur'an's own standard

The Qur'an tells believers to **verify** news brought by a wrongdoer: *"If a wrongdoer brings you news, verify it"* (49:6). The condition implies that news from a trustworthy person need not be treated with the same suspicion. The Qur'an also builds legal judgments on the testimony of a small number of reliable witnesses (2:282; 65:2), and says that *"a group"* from each community should learn the religion and return to *"warn their people"* (9:122).

## What about "conjecture"?

Critics cite verses such as *"They follow nothing but conjecture (zann), and conjecture avails nothing against the truth"* (53:28). But the Arabic word *zann* takes its meaning from context.

- In 53:28 it describes **unfounded** belief, the pagans' guesses about angels and idols.
- In 2:45–46 the humble are those *"who are certain (yazunnun) that they will meet their Lord."* Al-Qurtubi and others explain *zann* here as **conviction**.
- In 69:20 the believer says, *"I was certain (zanantu) that I would meet my account."* Al-Shinqiti, in *Adwa' al-Bayan*, lists such uses.

So the Qur'an's criticism of *zann* is a criticism of baseless guessing. It is not a rejection of well-grounded judgment.

## Action and certainty

Classical scholars distinguished two questions:

1. **Is the evidence enough to act on?** For a sound *ahad* report, the great majority held that it is. Ibn Hazm, in *al-Ihkam*, argued at length that it gives knowledge. Others held that it gives strong probability, which is enough for action, as courts act on witnesses.
2. **Does it give certainty?** Many held that a sound report supported by surrounding evidence, such as its acceptance by the scholars, multiple chains, or its inclusion in the two Sahih collections, can give certainty. Others reserved certainty for mass transmission.

Either way, the conclusion is not that *ahad* reports are worthless. It is that **each report has to be assessed on its own**: its transmitters, its chain, its wording and its relation to other evidence. See [how a report is judged sound](article.html?id=101).
