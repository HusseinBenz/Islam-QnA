This may be the hardest question anyone asks about God, and it is often asked through tears rather than in a seminar. No single sentence answers it. Islamic teaching offers several connected answers, each of which addresses part of the problem.

## 1. Much suffering comes from human choice

A great deal of pain is caused by people: war, cruelty, exploitation, neglect. The Qur'an attributes it plainly: *"Corruption has appeared on land and sea because of what people's hands have earned"* (30:41).

A world with genuine moral choice is a world in which people can choose badly. If God prevented every harmful choice the moment it was made, there would be no real responsibility, no real courage, generosity or justice, because none of these can exist where harm is impossible. The person who abuses freedom is responsible for the harm, not the One who gave freedom.

Islam also commands people to resist that harm. The Prophet ﷺ said: *"There should be neither harm nor reciprocating harm"* (Sunan Ibn Majah 2341), and *"Whoever among you sees a wrong, let him change it with his hand; if he cannot, then with his tongue; if he cannot, then with his heart"* (Sahih Muslim 49).

## 2. Life is a test, and hardship is not wasted

Human choice cannot explain illness, earthquakes or the death of a child. Here the Qur'an speaks of trial:

> *"Every soul will taste death. And We test you with evil and with good as a trial, and to Us you will be returned."* (Quran 21:35)

> *"He who created death and life to test you, which of you is best in deed."* (Quran 67:2)

Hardship in this view is not a sign that God has abandoned someone. The Prophet ﷺ said: *"No fatigue, illness, worry, grief, harm or distress befalls a Muslim, not even a thorn that pricks him, but God expiates some of his sins by it"* (Sahih al-Bukhari 5641; Sahih Muslim 2573). Asked who are tested most severely, he said: *"The prophets, then those most like them, then those most like them"* (Jami' al-Tirmidhi 2398). Hardship can bring patience, compassion, humility and a return to God that ease rarely produces.

## 3. This life is not the whole story

If death were the end, many sufferings would remain unredeemed forever. Islam teaches that it is not the end:

> *"Whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it."* (Quran 99:7–8)

Those who escaped justice in this life will face it, and losses that seemed irreparable will be made good. The Prophet ﷺ described the person who suffered most in this world being dipped once into Paradise and asked, *"Did you ever see any hardship?"* He replies, *"No, my Lord. I never experienced hardship"* (Sahih Muslim 2807). This is a claim of faith, but it changes the arithmetic of suffering entirely.

## 4. A wider view can reveal hidden wisdom

In the Qur'an's story of Moses and al-Khidr (18:65–82), Moses watches a boat being damaged and objects. He later learns that a ruler was seizing every sound boat, and the damage protected the owners' livelihood. Ibn Kathir and other commentators draw the lesson that events which look purely harmful may serve purposes we cannot see from where we stand. Ibn al-Qayyim devoted long chapters of *Shifa' al-'Alil* to the wisdom in God's creating what we experience as evil. Evil is never willed for its own sake, but it is permitted within a whole that is good. See [trusting God's wisdom](article.html?id=59).

## Room for grief and for action

None of these answers asks anyone to pretend. The Prophet ﷺ wept at the death of his son (Sahih al-Bukhari 1303). Muslims are commanded to relieve suffering, heal the sick and stop injustice, not to watch it with folded arms. These answers explain why suffering does not disprove a good God. They are not reasons to accept preventable suffering.
