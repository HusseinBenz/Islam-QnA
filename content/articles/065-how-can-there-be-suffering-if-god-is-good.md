Some pain comes from people’s choices. The book argues that allowing genuine choices makes moral responsibility possible. A person who abuses that freedom is responsible for the harm. Illness and earthquakes raise another question, since human choice cannot explain every kind of suffering.

The Qur’an presents this life as a place where people are tested by good and hardship (21:35). Al-Sayyid says hardship can sometimes lead to growth, change or forgiveness. He also appeals to an afterlife where those who escape accountability in this life can face judgment and harms that seemed irreparable can be made right. These are claims of Islamic faith.

In the Qur’an, Moses sees a boat damaged and objects. He later learns that a ruler was seizing sound boats and the damage was meant to protect the owners’ livelihood. The story illustrates how a wider view can change our understanding of an event. The author uses it to argue that goodness and wisdom can be present beyond what we can observe, including in the afterlife. That leaves real room for grief and for opposing preventable harm.
