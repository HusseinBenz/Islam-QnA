"A woman gets half of what a man gets" is often presented as the whole of Islamic inheritance law. It describes some cases well and many cases not at all. The shares are set out in a few verses of Surat al-Nisa' (4:11–12, 4:176), and their outcomes change with the family the deceased leaves behind.

## Where the two-to-one ratio applies

> *"God instructs you concerning your children: for the male, the like of the share of two females."* (Quran 4:11)

When a son and a daughter inherit together, the son receives twice the daughter's share. The same ratio applies to full or paternal brothers and sisters inheriting together (4:176). These are the cases most people have in mind.

## Where it does not

The same passage, and the scholars' application of it, give very different results in other configurations:

- **Equal shares.** If the deceased leaves children, **each parent receives one sixth**, mother and father alike (4:11). Half-siblings through the mother **share equally, male and female** (4:12).
- **A woman receives more.** A lone daughter receives **half** of the estate (4:11). If a woman dies leaving her husband and one daughter, the husband receives a quarter and the daughter half (4:11–12). If a man dies leaving a daughter, his mother and his father, the daughter receives half, while the father receives a sixth plus the small remainder. The daughter receives more than her grandfather.
- **A woman inherits where a man does not.** If a man dies leaving a daughter, a full sister and a paternal half-brother, the daughter takes half and the full sister takes the rest. The half-brother receives nothing, on the majority view recorded by Ibn Qudama in *al-Mughni*.

The determining factors are **the degree of relationship**, **the generation** (descendants who are starting families generally receive more than ascendants), and **the financial responsibilities** that each relative bears. Sex is not the only factor, and often it is not decisive.

## Why the two-to-one cases?

Classical commentators, including Ibn Kathir and al-Qurtubi, explain the two-to-one cases by the different financial duties Islamic law places on men and women:

- A man must give his bride a **bridal gift** (*mahr*), which is hers alone (4:4).
- A husband must **support his wife and children**, with housing, food and clothing, *"according to his means"* (65:7), even if his wife is wealthy.
- A woman's inheritance, earnings and property **remain hers**. She is not obliged to spend them on the household.

On this reading, a son's larger share comes with obligations his sister does not carry. A daughter's share is hers to keep, while much of her brother's is committed in advance to others. The rationale is that rights and duties should be weighed together, not one share in isolation.

## A fair question

A reader may still ask whether that rationale holds in a modern household where both spouses earn. That is a fair question about the fit between a legal system and changing social conditions. It should be asked about the **whole** system, not one share. Islamic law also leaves room for gifts during life and for a bequest of up to one third of the estate to non-heirs. For the main case of daughters and sons, see [why a daughter inherits half a son's share](article.html?id=32).
