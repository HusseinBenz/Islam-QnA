The Qur'an praises people who combine remembrance with reflection:

> *"Indeed, in the creation of the heavens and the earth and the alternation of night and day are signs for those of understanding, who remember God standing, sitting and lying on their sides, and reflect on the creation of the heavens and the earth: 'Our Lord, You did not create this in vain.'"* (Quran 3:190–191)

The verse joins two things that are often separated. One is **thinking** about the evidence. The other is **living** with God. An informed faith needs both. Knowledge without worship tends to become dry, and worship without knowledge tends to be fragile when a hard question arrives. Six habits help hold them together.

## 1. Look closely at the natural world

The Qur'an tells its readers to look again at the sky, the earth, the rain, the camel and their own bodies (21:32; 51:20–21; 88:17–20). Attending to how something works, such as the development of an embryo, the water cycle or the structure of a cell, turns a familiar scene back into a sign. A good documentary or a short course in a science you know little about can do this. The Qur'an promises: *"We will show them Our signs in the horizons and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that it is the truth"* (41:53).

## 2. Read the Qur'an for its argument

Read a passage with one question in mind: *what reason does it give?* Trace how it connects the purpose of life, resurrection or God's attributes to the evidence it offers. Use a reliable commentary when you need context. The Qur'an asks for this kind of reading: *"Do they not reflect upon the Qur'an?"* (47:24). Muhammad Abdullah Draz's *The Qur'an: An Eternal Challenge* is an excellent guide to the Qur'an's structure and its case for its own origin.

## 3. Study the foundations

Read at least one serious work on each of three questions: the case for God, the origin of the Qur'an, and the evidence for Muhammad's prophethood. Muslims have written on these for a thousand years. Al-Khattabi, al-Rummani, al-Baqillani and 'Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani wrote on the Qur'an's literary inimitability. Abu Nu'aym al-Isfahani, Qadi 'Abd al-Jabbar and al-Bayhaqi (*Dala'il al-Nubuwwa*) gathered the signs of prophethood. A reading group that summarises and compares the arguments makes this easier.

## 4. Learn who God is

Muslims know *that* they must worship God alone, but often know less about *who* God is as the Qur'an describes Him: creator, sustainer, all-knowing, merciful, just. The first revelation begins exactly here: *"Read in the name of your Lord who created, created man from a clinging substance"* (96:1–2). Studying God's names and the verses that mention them, such as the Throne Verse (2:255) or the end of Surat al-Hashr (59:22–24), deepens both understanding and love.

## 5. Practise the inner acts of worship

Trust, humility, gratitude, love, fear and hope are acts of the heart. Ibn al-Qayyim's *Madarij al-Salikin* maps them in detail. They are not only feelings. They are forms of knowledge that come from living with God. Abraham's reply to his people shows conviction of this kind: *"Which of the two parties has more right to security, if you know? Those who believe and do not mix their belief with injustice, they will have security"* (6:81–82).

## 6. Listen to people who came to Islam later

Converts often notice what lifelong Muslims take for granted. Muhammad Asad's *The Road to Mecca* and *The Autobiography of Malcolm X* describe very different journeys. Heraclius's questioning of Abu Sufyan, in which a foreign ruler reasoned his way to respect for the Prophet from his character and his followers (Sahih al-Bukhari 7), shows the same thing from the seventh century. Personal testimony does not prove a claim, but it can bring a familiar teaching back into focus.
