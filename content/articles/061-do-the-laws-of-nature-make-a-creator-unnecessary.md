The equations of aerodynamics explain how a plane stays in the air. Knowing them perfectly would still leave unanswered how that plane came to exist: who designed it, built it and fuelled it. An explanation can do two different jobs, and it helps to keep them apart.

## What a law of nature does

In science, a law describes a stable pattern. It tells you what will happen under certain conditions, and lets you predict and explain particular events. Newton's law of gravitation, for example, lets astronomers predict an orbit to great precision.

What a law does **not** do is bring anything into existence. A law of gravity describes how masses attract. It does not produce the masses. A law of motion describes how a moving body behaves. It does not start anything moving. The law is a description of regularity in things that already exist.

## The real question

So the philosophical question is not "Are there laws?" but:

- **Why is there a universe at all, with matter and energy for laws to describe?**
- **Why does that universe follow stable, mathematical regularities, rather than being chaotic?**
- **Why are those regularities ones that human minds can discover?**

Describing the regularities, however completely, does not answer these questions. Some writers have suggested that a law such as gravity could let a universe create itself from nothing (see [can science answer every question?](article.html?id=121)). But a law of gravity presupposes something for gravity to act on, and the "nothing" in such proposals usually turns out to be a physical state governed by laws. That is not nothing.

## How the Qur'an sees natural order

The Qur'an does not set God against the laws of nature. It presents natural regularity as **evidence** of God:

> *"The sun and the moon move by precise calculation."* (Quran 55:5)

> *"It is not for the sun to overtake the moon, nor does the night outstrip the day. Each is swimming in an orbit."* (Quran 36:40)

It calls God's consistent way of acting His *sunna*, His "custom" or "practice": *"You will not find any change in the way of God"* (33:62; 35:43). Ibn Kathir explains that the precise calculation of 55:5 means the sun and moon run according to a fixed, measured order that neither breaks nor varies. On this view, regularity is exactly what we should expect from a wise Creator. The laws of nature are His habits of action, not His rivals.

## Causes in Islamic thought

Muslim theologians discussed natural causation carefully, and they did not all agree. Al-Ghazali and the Ash'ari school held that God creates each effect directly at the occasion of its cause: fire does not burn by its own power, but God creates burning when fire touches cotton. Ibn Taymiyya, and many others, held that God gives created things **real causal powers**, which operate by His will. Fire really burns, and it burns because God made it so.

Both positions agree on the point that matters here. Natural causes, however real and regular, do not make God unnecessary, because they themselves exist and operate only through Him. A world of real causes still needs an explanation of why it exists and why its causes work as they do.

## The analogy and its limits

The plane analogy clarifies the question but does not settle it. That the universe needs an ultimate explanation, and what kind of explanation could answer it, is supported by independent arguments: from [origin, order and providence](article.html?id=58), and from the fact that [a chain of dependent causes needs a foundation](article.html?id=60). The point here is simpler. The laws of nature, by themselves, cannot do that job.
