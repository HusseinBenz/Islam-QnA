Al-Sayyid develops the idea in three ways. **Basic reasoning.** We grasp that a window is smaller than the building it belongs to, even without seeing it. We also naturally look for a cause when something begins. The author takes these basic judgments as tools for thinking about a creator and as gifts worth explaining in their own right.

**Dependence and worship.** Across many cultures, people have turned to worship and sought help beyond themselves, especially in hardship. The Quran presents prophets as reminding their audiences of a creator and calling them to worship God alone. For Al-Sayyid, upbringing and social pressures can change how the disposition is expressed. People can also differ about whether such a disposition is universal or what best explains it.

**Instinct and conscience.** The book gives examples such as a newborn’s ability to suckle, attachment between parent and child, and the intuition that cruelty calls for moral judgment. The thinker Abdelwahab Elmessiri asked whether a strictly material account could exhaust the meaning of parental love. Al-Sayyid takes these observations as clues that make sense within belief in God. This is a philosophical and theological reading of familiar experiences.
