"I know of no disagreement on this" tells you what a writer has found. "There is a consensus on this" makes a much stronger claim: that **all** the qualified scholars held the same view. The difference between those two statements is the key to one of the most quoted sayings of Imam Ahmad ibn Hanbal.

## The statement

Ahmad's son 'Abd Allah reports his father as saying, in substance:

> *"Whoever claims consensus is lying. Perhaps the people disagreed. This is the claim of Bishr al-Marisi and al-Asamm. Rather, let him say: 'We do not know that people disagreed,' or 'It has not reached me.'"*

Quoted on its own, the first sentence sounds like a rejection of consensus altogether. Modern writers have sometimes used it that way, to argue that no claim of scholarly agreement can be trusted.

## Ahmad himself appealed to consensus

That reading runs into an obvious problem: Ahmad appealed to consensus himself. Abu Dawud al-Sijistani, in his collection of questions put to Ahmad (*Masa'il al-Imam Ahmad*), records him citing agreement that Qur'an 7:204 (*"When the Qur'an is recited, listen to it and be silent"*) was revealed concerning prayer. Other reports from Ahmad cite consensus on other questions. A scholar who rejected all consensus would not argue this way.

## The fuller statement explains the target

The fuller version names its target: **Bishr al-Marisi and al-Asamm**. Both were theologians of the Mu'tazili and related currents, known for asserting "consensus" in support of their positions. In Ahmad's view they did so without real knowledge of the views of the Companions and their successors.

## How Hanbali scholars explained it

Later scholars of Ahmad's own school addressed the statement directly:

- **Abu Ya'la ibn al-Farra'**, in *al-'Udda fi Usul al-Fiqh*, explained it as a warning against **overclaiming**: against declaring consensus on the strength of not having heard of a disagreement, especially by someone who had not surveyed the early views. It was a demand for scholarly caution, not a denial that consensus exists or binds.
- **Ibn Rajab**, as quoted by al-Mardawi in *al-Tahbir*, connected it with the claims of theologians who did not know the earlier positions well.
- **The Taymiyya family's *al-Musawwada*** records the same understanding.
- **Ibn al-Qayyim**, in *al-Sawa'iq al-Mursala*, connected it with a further misuse: appealing to an alleged consensus in order to **reject sound hadith**. Ahmad, a hadith scholar above all, opposed this.

## The practical lesson

Ahmad's statement is best read as a rule of method. Before saying "the scholars agree," check that you actually know what the scholars said. That rule is still worth following:

- A **documented** consensus, such as that the daily prescribed prayers are five, is strong evidence.
- A claim of consensus that rests on **not knowing of disagreement** is weaker, and should be stated as such.
- Claims differ in strength **even for scholars who fully accept** the authority of a proven consensus ([see here](article.html?id=103)).

So Ahmad did not reject consensus. He rejected careless claims of it.
