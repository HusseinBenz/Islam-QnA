The Qur'an says:

> *"There is no compulsion in religion. The right course has become clear from the wrong."* (Quran 2:256)

A report in Sahih al-Bukhari records the Prophet ﷺ saying: *"Whoever changes his religion, kill him"* (Sahih al-Bukhari 6922). Another says that a Muslim's blood is lawful only in three cases, one of which is *"the one who abandons his religion and separates from the community"* (Sahih al-Bukhari 6878; Sahih Muslim 1676). Ibn Qudama records in *al-Mughni* that the jurists he discusses agreed on the general rule.

How can both stand together? Classical scholars did not overlook the question.

## How the early interpreters read "no compulsion"

Al-Tabari collects the early explanations of 2:256 in his commentary. They fall into two main groups:

- Some held the verse applies to **the People of the Book and others who accept the *jizya* arrangement**. Such communities may not be forced into Islam and keep their own religion under Muslim rule.
- Others reported that the verse was revealed about children of the Ansar who had been raised as Jews. When the Jewish tribe of Banu al-Nadir was expelled, some parents wanted to force them into Islam, and the verse forbade it.

Al-Tabari himself favoured the first reading. Ibn Kathir explains the verse as meaning that nobody is to be compelled to enter Islam, because its truth is clear. On both readings, "no compulsion" concerns **entering** the religion.

## How they read the apostasy ruling

The jurists treated **leaving** Islam, after entering it knowingly and freely, as a different legal category. They read the phrase *"and separates from the community"* as showing that the issue was understood together with **loyalty to the community**, in a world where religion and political allegiance were bound together. Leaving the faith often meant joining its enemies. The Qur'an itself mentions a tactic of declaring belief in the morning and disbelief in the evening in order to unsettle the believers (3:72).

The jurists also surrounded the ruling with conditions:

- **Repentance must be offered first.** 'Umar ibn al-Khattab is reported to have objected when an apostate was executed without being given three days to reconsider (recorded in the *Musannaf* of 'Abd al-Razzaq and in Malik's *Muwatta'*).
- **The person must be an adult of sound mind**, acting freely, and the matter must be established before a court.
- **Women:** the Hanafi school held that a woman who leaves Islam is not executed.
- **Indefinite opportunity:** some early authorities, including Ibrahim al-Nakha'i and Sufyan al-Thawri, are reported to have held that the apostate should be invited to repent indefinitely. The *Musannaf* of 'Abd al-Razzaq records their view.

Only a legitimate court could apply the ruling, never an individual.

## Why is this still debated?

Readers today raise freedom of conscience, tolerance, and episodes from the Prophet's life in which people who left Islam were not pursued. They also note that the Qur'an describes people who *"believed, then disbelieved, then believed, then disbelieved"* (4:137) and speaks of their fate in the Hereafter without prescribing a worldly penalty.

Within Muslim scholarship, there are several positions:

- Many scholars defend the classical position as established by sound reports and early practice.
- Some modern scholars argue that the ruling concerned apostasy **combined with rebellion or treason** against the community, citing "separates from the community." On this view it should not apply to private change of belief.
- Some discuss whether it is a fixed penalty (*hadd*) or a discretionary one, a classification that affects how it may be applied.

Part of the disagreement is a deeper difference over the place of divine command and individual autonomy ([see here](article.html?id=47)). Part of it is a disagreement over the texts and over the reach of historical state authority. Explaining the classical interpretation accurately helps make clear which disagreement is which. Careful reading on all sides serves the discussion better than slogans.
