The Prophet ﷺ married more women than the Qur'an permits other Muslim men. Critics see this as self-interest. The sources themselves give a more detailed picture, and it is worth reading them case by case.

## The general rule and the Prophet's exception

For Muslims generally, the Qur'an sets a limit and a condition:

> *"Marry those that please you of women, two or three or four. But if you fear that you will not be just, then only one."* (Quran 4:3)

Fair treatment is the condition. If a man fears he cannot be fair, he should marry one. See [why Islam allows polygamy](article.html?id=31).

A separate passage addresses the Prophet's own household (33:50–52). It names the categories of women lawful for him, and then sets a limit: *"Not lawful to you are women after this, nor to exchange them for other wives, even if their beauty pleases you"* (33:52). Islamic legal tradition treats these verses as rulings specific to him, not as a model for other men. The same passage forbade his wives to remarry after him (33:53).

## A long monogamous marriage first

The Prophet married Khadija when he was about twenty-five and she was about forty, a widow and a successful merchant. They were married for about twenty-five years, until her death, and he took no other wife during that time. Years later Aisha said she was never as jealous of any woman as she was of Khadija, because of how often the Prophet remembered her (Sahih al-Bukhari 3818). His later marriages took place in the final decade of his life, in Medina, mostly after he was over fifty.

## Different reasons in different cases

The sources link particular marriages to particular circumstances:

- **Sawda**, a widow who had emigrated to Abyssinia, whom he married after Khadija's death.
- **Hafsa**, daughter of 'Umar, widowed when her husband died of wounds after Badr. 'Umar offered her in marriage to Abu Bakr and 'Uthman before the Prophet proposed (Sahih al-Bukhari 4005).
- **Umm Salama**, widowed with young children. When her husband died, the Prophet taught her to pray, *"O God, reward me in my affliction and give me something better in its place."* She said, "Who could be better than Abu Salama?" Then the Prophet proposed (Sahih Muslim 918).
- **Zaynab bint Jahsh**, previously married to Zayd, the Prophet's adopted son. The Qur'an presents this marriage as abolishing the pre-Islamic custom that treated an adopted son as a biological son in law: *"so that there be no difficulty for the believers concerning the wives of their adopted sons"* (33:37; see also 33:4–5, 33:40).
- **Juwayriya**, daughter of the chief of Banu al-Mustaliq, captured in battle. When the Companions learned of the marriage, they freed the captives of her tribe as the Prophet's in-laws. Aisha said she knew of no woman who was a greater blessing to her people (Sunan Abi Dawud 3931).
- **Umm Habiba**, daughter of Abu Sufyan, then the leader of Mecca's opposition, who had remained Muslim in Abyssinia after her husband left Islam.
- **Safiyya**, freed after Khaybar ([see here](article.html?id=85)).

Of his wives, Aisha was the only one not previously married. See [what the sources say about Aisha](article.html?id=11).

## What the sources support, and what they do not

The cases show recurring themes: care for widows of men who died in the community's struggles, ties of kinship with key Companions and tribes, legal reform, and the freeing of captives. They also show that the marriages were real marriages, with affection, disagreement and family life. The Qur'an itself records tensions in the household (66:1–5).

**A single explanation for every marriage would go beyond what the sources can establish.** Claims that each marriage was purely political, or purely charitable, are as unsupported as the claim that they were self-indulgent. The honest approach is to read each case on its own evidence.
