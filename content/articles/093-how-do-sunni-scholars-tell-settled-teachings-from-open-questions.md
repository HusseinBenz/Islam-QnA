Two people can ask about the same hadith and have completely different questions. One wants to know whether the words really go back to Muhammad. Another accepts that they do, but wants to know what they mean or how they apply. A third doubts whether any hadith should count at all. Treating these as one question leads to confusion.

## Three separate questions

Sunni scholarship distinguishes:

1. **Authority.** Does this kind of source carry weight at all? For example, is the Sunnah a source of guidance alongside the Qur'an? ([See here](article.html?id=96).)
2. **Authenticity.** Is this particular report reliably traced to the Prophet? ([See here](article.html?id=101).)
3. **Interpretation.** What does it mean, how does it relate to other texts, and how does it apply? ([See here](article.html?id=105).)

The same distinction matters beyond hadith. A dispute about a reported miracle or about life after death may turn on **authenticity**. A dispute about a legal rule may turn on **interpretation**, or on whether scholars really agreed on it.

## Certain and probable

Works of legal theory, such as al-Ghazali's *al-Mustasfa*, classify evidence by how certain it is in two respects:

- **Certainty of transmission** (*qat'i al-thubut*). The Qur'an and mass-transmitted reports are certain. Most individual hadith are probable to varying degrees.
- **Certainty of meaning** (*qat'i al-dalala*). Some texts have one clear meaning. Others can reasonably bear more than one.

A teaching is **settled** (*thabit*, *qat'i*) when its source is certain and its meaning clear, or when a verified **consensus** (*ijma'*) confirms it ([see here](article.html?id=103)). Examples are the oneness of God, the prophethood of Muhammad, the five daily prayers, the fast of Ramadan, and the prohibition of wine, theft and murder.

Where either the transmission or the meaning is probable, scholars may reach different conclusions. Examples include the details of how to pray, many questions of trade, and the precise conditions of a ruling. That is the space of **legitimate disagreement** (*ikhtilaf*).

## Universal principles and particular rulings

Al-Shatibi, in *al-Muwafaqat*, argued that the law's universal principles are established with certainty. They are known from the agreement of many texts together, not from any single one. They include the protection of religion, life, intellect, lineage and property, the removal of hardship, and justice. A single probable text cannot overturn a principle that many texts together establish. This is one reason a settled teaching is not shaken by one difficult report.

## Disagreement is not a defect

The Prophet ﷺ said: *"When a judge strives to reach a judgment and is right, he has two rewards. When he strives and errs, he has one reward"* (Sahih al-Bukhari 7352). Honest effort in an open question is rewarded even when it misses.

Ibn Taymiyya wrote a treatise defending the great imams against the charge of ignoring hadith (*Raf' al-Malam 'an al-A'imma al-A'lam*, in *Majmu' al-Fatawa*, vol. 20). He explained that a scholar might not have received a report, might have judged its chain weak, might have understood it differently, or might have considered it superseded. Disagreement, on this view, usually has reasons that can be examined.

## Why this matters for hard questions

When a question about Islam comes up, it helps to ask first: **is this about a settled teaching or an open question?**

- If it is settled, the discussion is about the evidence for the foundation itself.
- If it is open, the right answer may be "Muslim scholars differ, and here are the main views and their reasons."

Presenting an open question as settled, or a settled teaching as merely one opinion, misrepresents the tradition. Identifying the disagreement accurately is part of explaining the issue.
