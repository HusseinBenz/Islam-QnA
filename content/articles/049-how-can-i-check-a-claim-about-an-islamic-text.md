Claims about Islamic texts usually fail in one of three ways. **The report may not be established.** **The text may be real but misunderstood.** Or **the text may be real and understood, but used to support a conclusion that goes beyond it.** Checking a claim means asking each of these in turn.

## Step one: check the report

Early Muslim collections recorded many reports, strong and weak, so that later scholars could assess them. Appearing in a book is not the same as being authentic. Al-Tabari says plainly in the preface to his *History* that he passes on what he received and that responsibility lies with those who transmitted it.

Several reports are often used to discredit the Companions or the Sunnah itself. Examples are a claim that 'Umar accused Abu Hurayra of theft, a claim that 'Umar burned written collections of hadith, a story about Abu Hurayra attached to the saying "Visit seldom and love will grow," and reports alleging that the Prophet cursed Mu'awiya. These reports appear in early works such as Ibn Sa'd's *Tabaqat*, the *Musannaf* of 'Abd al-Razzaq, and the *Musnad* collections of al-Bazzar and al-Tayalisi (see the references).

When their chains are examined, they prove weak or unconnected. One example: the report that 'Umar ordered written hadith to be burned is transmitted by al-Qasim ibn Muhammad, grandson of Abu Bakr. Al-Qasim was born years after 'Umar's death, so he could not have witnessed it, and the report gives no source between them. Hadith critics call such a report *munqati'* ("broken"), and they do not treat it as established.

Context matters too. Muslim records an authentic report in which the Prophet said of the young Mu'awiya, *"May God not fill his stomach."* He places it immediately after the Prophet's prayer that any harsh word he spoke to someone who did not deserve it be turned into *"purification and mercy"* for that person (Sahih Muslim 2603–2604). Read in its context, the report shows how the early compilers themselves understood it.

## Step two: check what follows

Even a sound text has to be read accurately.

**Example: "We have neglected nothing in the Book."** Some argue from Qur'an 6:38 that the Qur'an contains everything, so the Sunnah is unnecessary. Read the whole verse: *"There is no creature on earth, nor bird that flies with its wings, but they are communities like you. We have not neglected anything in the Book; then to their Lord they will be gathered."* The verse is about creatures and God's knowledge of them. Many commentators, as al-Tabari and Ibn Kathir record, understood "the Book" here as God's record of all things (the Preserved Tablet). Qur'an 16:89, which calls the revealed Book *"a clarification of all things,"* is a separate verse, and the scholars explained it as clarifying everything of guidance, partly by directing people to the Prophet's teaching. See [why Muslims read the Sunnah](article.html?id=94).

**Example: "Do not write from me."** Sahih Muslim 3004 records the Prophet saying: *"Do not write anything from me except the Qur'an; whoever has written anything other than the Qur'an should erase it. **And narrate from me, there is no harm in that.** Whoever lies about me deliberately, let him take his seat in the Fire."* The same hadith that discourages writing expressly commands oral transmission. Using it to deny the authority of hadith also relies on a hadith to make the case. See [did Muhammad forbid writing down hadith?](article.html?id=99)

## Step three: separate kinds of argument

If someone cites a text to persuade an audience that already accepts it, that is fair. If they cite it while denying its authority, they are not arguing from their own premises. Ask: **what does the person citing this actually accept as evidence?** Then judge the argument on those terms.

## A checklist

1. Find the text in its original collection, with its number or page.
2. Check how scholars graded it, and why.
3. Read the passage around it.
4. See how classical commentators understood it.
5. Ask whether the conclusion drawn actually follows.
