When a question troubles us about our own beliefs, it is tempting to compare our view, with all its difficulties in plain sight, against an alternative we have only seen at its most attractive. That is not a fair comparison. A fair one asks **both** views the same questions.

## The Qur'an's own tone

The Qur'an sometimes frames a dispute with striking even-handedness:

> *"Say: Who provides for you from the heavens and the earth? Say: God. And indeed, either we or you are upon guidance or in clear error."* (Quran 34:24)

Classical commentators, as Ibn Kathir notes, saw this as the speech of someone confident enough to let the evidence decide. It does not say "we are right and you are wrong." It invites the listener to weigh the matter. Elsewhere the Qur'an asks people to step away from the crowd to think: *"I advise you of one thing: that you stand for God, in pairs and individually, and then reflect"* (34:46).

## Three questions to put to both sides

**1. What happens to a wrong that no human court ever addresses?**
Many injustices are never put right: crimes never discovered, victims who die before seeing justice. Islam holds that there will be a judgment after death in which every right is restored: *"Whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it"* (99:7–8). A view without an afterlife can still condemn the harm and seek justice in this life, and many people who hold it do. But it has to accept that for many victims there will never be any justice at all. Ask which account fits better with the conviction, shared by almost everyone, that justice *matters*.

**2. Why is the world intelligible?**
Science explains a great many patterns, from inheritance to the regularities physicists describe with mathematics. It assumes, but does not explain, that the universe *has* stable, mathematical order that human minds can grasp. Why should that be? Theism says the world was made by a wise Creator and our minds were made to understand it. The Qur'an asks its readers to look and look again (67:3–4). Other views need their own explanation, or must treat intelligibility as a brute fact.

**3. What guidance did the Prophet leave, and how is it known?**
This question is internal to Muslims. Someone who decides to rely on "the Qur'an alone" should ask how that view understands the Qur'an's commands to *"obey the Messenger"* (4:59; 4:80) and *"whatever the Messenger gives you, take it"* (59:7). They should also ask how it explains the many practices, like the form of prayer, that Muslims have always learned through the Sunnah. See [why Muslims read the Sunnah](article.html?id=94).

## Method

1. **State each view in its strongest fair form**, in words its holders would accept.
2. **Ask the same questions of each.** Do not demand proof from one and accept assumptions from the other.
3. **Look at what each explains and what it leaves unexplained.** No worldview has zero difficulties. The question is which handles the evidence as a whole best.
4. **Write down what remains open**, and what evidence would change your mind.

## Leaving a view does not dissolve its question

A common experience is worth naming. Someone leaves a position because of a hard question. Then they find that the same question, or a harder one, waits in the new position. Someone troubled by suffering under belief in God may find that, without God, suffering raises questions about meaning and moral standards that are just as pressing. Changing position does not by itself resolve the issue that prompted the change. Only comparing the answers does.

The Qur'an's command for fairness applies here too: *"Do not let hatred of a people lead you to injustice. Be just; that is nearer to righteousness"* (5:8).
