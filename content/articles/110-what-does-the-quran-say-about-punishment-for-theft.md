> *"As for the thief, male or female, cut off their hands as a recompense for what they earned, an exemplary punishment from God. And God is Mighty and Wise. But whoever repents after his wrongdoing and reforms, God will turn to him in forgiveness. Indeed, God is Forgiving and Merciful."* (Quran 5:38–39)

The verse names a severe penalty. Its wording raises a question the classical commentators discussed in detail: **which acts count as "theft" (*sariqa*) for the purpose of this particular penalty?** Al-Tabari and al-Qurtubi, in their commentaries on 5:38, examine the Qur'anic wording alongside reports about which cases qualified. They record the jurists' disagreements on many details.

## The conditions

Drawing on the Sunnah and on the meaning of *sariqa* in Arabic (taking something secretly), the jurists set out conditions. Ibn Qudama gives them in *al-Mughni*. The main ones are:

1. **A minimum value (*nisab*).** The Prophet ﷺ said: *"The hand is cut off for a quarter of a dinar or more"* (Sahih al-Bukhari 6789; Sahih Muslim 1684). Below the minimum, the penalty does not apply. The schools differed on the exact amount.
2. **Taken by stealth.** Robbery by force, embezzlement of something entrusted, and snatching in public were classed differently. They were serious wrongs, but not *sariqa* in this technical sense.
3. **From a secured place (*hirz*).** Property left unguarded, or taken from where the taker had a right to be, did not qualify.
4. **No ownership share or doubt.** Taking from property in which one has a share, such as public funds according to some jurists, or a father taking from his son, averts the penalty.
5. **No genuine need.** 'Umar ibn al-Khattab is reported to have suspended the penalty during the year of famine. Jurists recognised that real hunger is a doubt that averts the penalty.
6. **Proof before a court**: two upright witnesses or a free, uncoerced confession, which can be withdrawn.
7. **Accountability**: the person must be an adult of sound mind, acting freely.

Any genuine doubt (*shubha*) averts the fixed penalty. The offence might then be dealt with by a lesser discretionary punishment, together with restitution to the owner.

## Equality before the law

When a woman from a noble clan, Banu Makhzum, was convicted of theft, people asked Usama ibn Zayd to intercede for her. The Prophet ﷺ was angered and said:

> *"Those before you were destroyed because when a noble among them stole, they let him go, and when a weak one stole, they applied the penalty to him. By God, if Fatima the daughter of Muhammad stole, I would cut off her hand."* (Sahih al-Bukhari 3475)

The point of the report is that **the law must apply equally to the powerful and the weak**.

## Forgiveness before the court

The Prophet ﷺ encouraged people to settle matters before they reached a judge: *"Forgive the fixed penalties among yourselves, for whatever reaches me of a fixed penalty becomes binding"* (Sunan Abi Dawud 4376). Once a case is properly before a court and proven, the penalty is not negotiable. Before that point, the victim may forgive.

## Repentance

Verse 39 joins the penalty to the possibility of **repentance and reform**. Jurists discussed whether repentance before arrest affects the penalty, and they agreed that it matters before God. The law's aim, as the verse presents it, includes deterrence (*"an exemplary punishment"*) and moral renewal.

## The ethical question

The conditions explain how classical courts understood and limited the rule. They do not remove the question many people ask about the severity of the penalty itself. That is a legitimate part of the discussion. It is best approached with an accurate picture of what the law required, not the caricature of a penalty applied to every petty theft.
