Imagine finding an unfamiliar part inside a machine that has worked flawlessly for years, a machine made by an engineer whose skill you have every reason to respect. You cannot tell what the part does. You would not conclude that it is pointless. You would conclude that you do not yet know its purpose. **Established knowledge guides your judgment when some details are unknown.**

Muslims trust God's wisdom in a similar way. This trust is not a substitute for reasons. It rests on them.

## Four steps to trust

**1. There are good reasons to believe in God.** The arguments from origin, order and providence ([see here](article.html?id=58)), along with the human [fitrah](article.html?id=57), point to a Creator.

**2. Creation shows His knowledge, power and wisdom.** A universe so finely ordered, from the structure of a cell to the regularity of the planets, reflects a Maker who knows what He is doing. The Qur'an asks: *"Did you think that We created you without purpose, and that you would not be returned to Us?"* (23:115). Ibn al-Qayyim wrote a whole book, *Shifa' al-'Alil*, defending the principle that all God's acts have wise purposes (*hikma*), even where we cannot see them.

**3. Human understanding is limited and keeps changing.** *"And you have not been given of knowledge except a little"* (17:85). Explanations that once seemed certain have been revised many times as new information arrived. It would be strange to treat our present inability to see a reason as proof that there is none.

**4. The prophets are trustworthy.** Muslims trust the prophets because of their character, the consistency of their message and the signs they brought ([see here](article.html?id=74)). That trust is why Muslims look to the Qur'an and the established Sunnah for insight into God's purposes, and accept guidance even where its full reasons are not stated.

## The story of Moses and al-Khidr

The Qur'an tells how Moses accompanied a servant of God, known in the tradition as al-Khidr, who had been given knowledge Moses lacked (18:65–82). Al-Khidr damaged a boat, killed a boy and repaired a wall without asking for payment, and Moses objected each time. Then the reasons came. A tyrant was seizing every sound ship, and the damaged one was spared. The boy would have brought grief and disbelief upon his believing parents. Under the wall lay a treasure belonging to two orphans, which would be kept safe for them until they grew up. Al-Khidr concludes: *"I did it not of my own accord"* (18:82).

Ibn Kathir and other commentators draw the lesson that events which look harmful may serve a wisdom that only appears later, or not in this life at all. The Qur'an says elsewhere: *"Perhaps you dislike something while it is good for you, and perhaps you love something while it is bad for you. God knows, and you do not know"* (2:216).

## Trust is not the same as silence

This approach applies **within** belief in God. Someone still deciding whether the starting points are sound should examine them in order, beginning with the first step, not the last.

Trust in God's wisdom also does not mean pretending not to hurt. When his infant son Ibrahim died, the Prophet ﷺ wept. Asked about his tears, he said: *"The eye weeps and the heart grieves, but we say only what pleases our Lord. We are saddened by your parting, O Ibrahim"* (Sahih al-Bukhari 1303). A person living through loss can ask *why*, and deserves care and company, even when the answer to why this happened remains unknown. See also [suffering and a good God](article.html?id=65).
