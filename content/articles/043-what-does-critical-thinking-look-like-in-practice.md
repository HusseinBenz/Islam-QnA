The Qur'an makes evidence a moral duty, not only an intellectual one:

> *"Do not pursue that of which you have no knowledge. Indeed, hearing, sight and the heart, each of these will be questioned."* (Quran 17:36)

> *"Say: Bring your proof, if you are truthful."* (Quran 2:111)

Critical thinking is simply taking those verses seriously in everyday life.

## A five-step test

Try this on the next claim about Islam (or anything else) that you see online.

1. **Write down exactly what it says.** Vague claims are hard to test. "Islam says X" and "Some scholar in the 12th century said X" are very different claims.
2. **Find the earliest source you can reach.** Not a screenshot, not a summary, but the verse, the hadith collection, the book and page.
3. **Ask how that source knows.** Was the author an eyewitness? Do they cite a chain or a document? How far are they from the event?
4. **Look for independent confirmation.** Does another source, which does not depend on the first, say the same thing?
5. **Ask whether the evidence supports the whole claim, or only part of it.** A real quotation can be used to support a conclusion it does not establish.

## Muslim scholars did this systematically

**Hadith criticism.** Early scholars insisted on knowing where a report came from. Muslim records in the introduction to his *Sahih* the words of Muhammad ibn Sirin: *"This knowledge is religion, so look carefully at whom you take your religion from."* He also records 'Abd Allah ibn al-Mubarak: *"The chain of transmission is part of religion. Were it not for the chain, anyone could say whatever he wished."*

Ibn al-Salah's classic handbook defines a sound (*sahih*) hadith by five conditions: an unbroken chain, every transmitter upright in character, every transmitter accurate in memory or writing, no contradiction of more reliable transmitters (*shudhudh*), and no hidden defect (*'illa*). In practice, scholars compared different versions of the same report, checked whether transmitters could have met, and tested even reliable-looking reports for errors. See [how a report is judged sound](article.html?id=101).

**The ethics of debate.** Muslim manuals of *adab al-bahth wa-l-munazara* (the etiquette of inquiry and debate) taught a principle that is still worth memorising: *"If you are transmitting, show that the transmission is accurate. If you are making a claim, show the evidence."* Al-Sabban's gloss on 'Adud al-Din al-Iji's treatise on debate is one of the standard places it is explained.

## Matching confidence to evidence

Hadith scholars did not sort reports into only "true" and "false." They graded them as sound, good, weak or fabricated, and a jurist's confidence in a ruling depended on that grade. The same habit is useful everywhere. Some things you can be certain of, some are probable, some are possible, and some are unsupported. Saying "I'm not sure" is part of thinking well, not a failure.

## Practise on ordinary things

You do not have to start with theology. Test claims from school, the news or a group chat. Use a library as well as a search engine. Learn how to look up a hadith by its collection and number (sunnah.com is a good start) before relying on someone's account of it. Over time you will not necessarily believe less. You will have **better reasons** each time you accept, question or set aside a claim. [How to check a claim about an Islamic text](article.html?id=49) applies these steps in detail.
