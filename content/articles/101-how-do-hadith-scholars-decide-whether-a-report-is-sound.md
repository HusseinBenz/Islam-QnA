Two honest people can remember the same event differently. A courtroom does not conclude that both are lying. It asks how the difference arose and what other evidence could settle it. Hadith criticism works the same way, applied to many thousands of reports and the people who carried them.

## The five conditions

Ibn al-Salah's *Introduction to the Science of Hadith*, the handbook on which most later works are built, defines a **sound** (*sahih*) hadith as one that meets five conditions:

1. **Integrity (*'adala*).** Every transmitter was honest, upright and known to fear God, with no record of lying.
2. **Accuracy (*dabt*).** Every transmitter usually preserved and conveyed what he or she received correctly, whether by memory or in a well-kept written record.
3. **Continuity (*ittisal*).** Each transmitter actually received the report from the one before, back to the Prophet. There are no missing links.
4. **No irregularity (*'adam al-shudhudh*).** The report does not contradict a more reliable version or more reliable transmitters.
5. **No hidden defect (*'adam al-'illa*).** Close comparison of versions reveals no subtle error in the chain or the wording.

The first three concern the **people and the chain**. The last two are discovered by **comparing versions**, and that is where the real expertise lies.

## How comparison works

Suppose a teacher had ten students, and nine of them report a hadith with one wording and the tenth with a different one. Critics would ask whether the tenth student was known for weak memory, or whether he heard the report at a different session. Depending on the answer, his version might be judged an error (*shadhdh*). In this way critics could often **locate an error at a particular narrator or link**, rather than rejecting a report wholesale.

The famous critics, such as 'Ali ibn al-Madini, Ahmad ibn Hanbal, al-Bukhari and al-Daraqutni, wrote whole books identifying hidden defects (*'ilal*) in reports that looked sound on the surface.

## Reliability is not all-or-nothing

A transmitter's reliability could vary:

- **By teacher.** A narrator might be precise when reporting from one teacher he studied with for years, and less precise with another he met briefly.
- **By period.** Some transmitters' memory declined in old age (*ikhtilat*). Critics then distinguished what students heard from them before and after the decline.
- **By medium.** Some were reliable when reading from their books and less so when reciting from memory.

Critics recorded these distinctions in detail, which is why a narrator may appear in one chain as reliable and in another as weak.

## Continuity: did they really meet?

To establish that a chain is unbroken, scholars needed to know whether each pair of transmitters met. Al-Bukhari is reported to have required evidence that they met at least once. Muslim, in the introduction to his *Sahih*, argued that it is enough that they were contemporaries and a meeting was possible, provided the narrator was not known for concealing gaps (*tadlis*). Works such as al-'Ala'i's *Jami' al-Tahsil* catalogue which transmitters heard from whom, and which reports have gaps.

## Grades

Reports were graded on a scale, not sorted into true and false:

- ***Sahih*** (sound): meets all five conditions fully.
- ***Hasan*** (good): meets them, but a transmitter's accuracy is somewhat lighter.
- ***Da'if*** (weak): fails one or more conditions.
- ***Mawdu'*** (fabricated): shown to be invented.

A weak report may be strengthened by other independent chains. Scholars may grade a report differently when they weigh the clues differently, and that disagreement is itself part of the discipline.
