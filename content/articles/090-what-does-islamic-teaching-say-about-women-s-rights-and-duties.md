Start with a concrete question, such as whether a family can arrange a marriage against a woman's wishes. In a report collected by al-Bukhari and Muslim, Muhammad directs that a previously married woman be consulted and that a woman marrying for the first time give her permission. The report describes silence as a possible response in the social setting it addresses. The question remains whether permission was freely given.

**Rights and duties work together.** The Quran connects worship of God with kindness to parents and draws attention to what a mother endures in pregnancy and nursing. In a hadith, Muhammad names the mother three times when asked who most deserves good companionship. In classical family law a husband owes a marriage gift and financial support even if his wife has wealth of her own.

**Why do some rules differ?** The book cites classical distinctions on military duty, congregational prayer, gold and silk, and the historical poll tax known as *jizya*. It argues that legal fairness can account for different obligations. It also asks readers to compare declarations of equality with actual opportunities, using access to public leadership as an example. That comparison gives no basis for judging a person's ability by sex.

The author also asks readers to consider responsibilities arising from sexual relationships, including the moral status of unborn life. That is an ethical concern. Claims about why abortions occur or how often they occur require evidence of their own.
