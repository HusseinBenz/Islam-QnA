Debates about women in Islam often swing between two slogans: "Islam oppresses women" and "Islam gave women all their rights." Neither helps much. It is more useful to start with concrete questions and look at what the sources actually say.

## Equal before God

The Qur'an addresses women and men together as moral agents:

> *"Indeed, the Muslim men and Muslim women, the believing men and believing women, the devout men and devout women... God has prepared for them forgiveness and a great reward."* (Quran 33:35)

> *"Whoever does righteousness, whether male or female, while being a believer, We will surely give them a good life."* (Quran 16:97)

The Prophet ﷺ said: *"Women are the counterparts (shaqa'iq) of men"* (Sunan Abi Dawud 236).

## A concrete test: can a woman be married against her will?

No. The Prophet ﷺ said: *"A previously married woman may not be married until she is consulted, and a virgin may not be married until her permission is sought"* (Sahih al-Bukhari 5136; Sahih Muslim 1419). The report notes that a shy young woman's silence could indicate consent in that social setting. The real question is whether permission was **freely given**. When Khansa' bint Khidham was married by her father against her wishes, she complained to the Prophet, and he **annulled the marriage** (Sahih al-Bukhari 5138).

## Rights that come with marriage

- **The bridal gift (*mahr*)** belongs to the wife, not her family: *"Give women their bridal gifts graciously"* (4:4).
- **Financial support (*nafaqa*)** is the husband's duty, for housing, food and clothing, *"according to his means"* (65:7). Ibn al-Mundhir records scholarly consensus on this, and Ibn Qudama's *al-Mughni* details it. The duty applies **even if the wife is wealthy**.
- **Her own property** remains hers. Classical jurists did not require a wife to spend her wealth on the household.
- **Kind treatment**: *"Live with them in kindness"* (4:19). *"The best of you are those best to their wives"* (Jami' al-Tirmidhi 3895).

## Mothers

The Qur'an joins the worship of God with kindness to parents (17:23), and draws special attention to what a mother endures: *"His mother carried him, weakness upon weakness"* (31:14). Asked who most deserves good companionship, the Prophet ﷺ said *"Your mother"* three times, and then *"your father"* (Sahih al-Bukhari 5971).

## Why do some rules differ?

Classical law assigns some duties differently. Women are not required to fight; Aisha reports the Prophet telling women that their best *jihad* is an accepted pilgrimage (Sahih al-Bukhari 2875). They are not required to attend congregational Friday prayer. Gold and silk are permitted to women and not to men. Jurists held that the *jizya*, the historical tax on non-Muslim men of fighting age, was not levied on women (Ibn al-Qayyim, *Ahkam Ahl al-Dhimma*). Ibn Battal's commentary on al-Bukhari discusses several of these distinctions.

The classical argument is that **legal fairness is judged by the whole set of rights and duties**, not by one rule in isolation. A wife who receives a bridal gift and full support, and owes no military service, stands in a different position from a husband who owes all of these. The [inheritance rules](article.html?id=91) are an example.

Two cautions follow. First, these are claims about how a legal system distributes obligations, and they can be examined on their merits. Second, they give **no basis for judging any individual's ability by sex**. Women in early Islam included scholars like Aisha, to whom senior Companions brought their questions, and businesswomen like Khadija, who employed the young Muhammad in her trade.

## Comparing claims with reality

It is fair to compare any system's declarations of equality with the actual opportunities it gives women. That applies to Muslim societies, many of which fall short of their own tradition. It applies equally to societies that declare equality but leave women underrepresented in leadership, underpaid, or exposed to exploitation.

## A related ethical concern

Islamic ethics also asks about responsibilities arising from sexual relationships, including the moral status of unborn life. The Qur'an forbids killing children *"out of fear of poverty"* (17:31). Claims about how often and why abortions occur are separate empirical questions and need their own evidence.
