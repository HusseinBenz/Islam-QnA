Being coerced feels very different from deciding freely. That distinction matters to the book’s answer. The Qur’an speaks of people willing to take a path and of their will depending on God (76:29–30). A person’s intentions and decisions are part of how the action occurs, and therefore part of what can make the person responsible.

Al-Sayyid compares willing an action to other causes in the world. Water relieves thirst and fire ordinarily burns. In a theistic account, these processes operate within God’s will. The Qur’anic account of Abraham’s fire illustrates the belief that God can suspend an ordinary effect (21:69). In the same way, a human decision has a real role even within a world God sustains.

The book also points to the purpose of sending messengers and revealing guidance. Commands and warnings presuppose that people can respond. It accepts both claims while leaving the full relation between human choice and divine decree among the questions human understanding cannot exhaust.
