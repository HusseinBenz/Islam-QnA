If God already knows and has written everything I will do, am I really choosing? And if I am not, how can I be judged? People have asked this since the Prophet's own time. Islamic theology does not dissolve the mystery completely, but it does give a coherent answer.

## The Qur'an affirms both

> *"So whoever wills may take a way to his Lord. And you do not will except that God wills."* (Quran 76:29–30)

> *"For whoever among you wills to go straight. And you do not will except that God wills, Lord of the worlds."* (Quran 81:28–29)

> *"And say: The truth is from your Lord, so whoever wills, let him believe, and whoever wills, let him disbelieve."* (Quran 18:29)

The Qur'an never asks the reader to choose between human will and divine will. It states both, often in the same breath: **people really will, and their willing does not happen outside God's will.**

## Choosing is not the same as being forced

Everyone knows the difference between being pushed down the stairs and deciding to walk down them, or between signing a contract freely and signing at gunpoint. We do not hold someone responsible for the first, and we do for the second. Islamic law draws exactly this line. A person who is coerced, asleep, a child, or without sound mind is not accountable for what they do in that state.

A person's intentions and decisions are part of **how** an action happens. They are what make it *his* action and not merely an event that befell him. That is the basis of responsibility.

## Decree works through causes, including our choices

Ibn al-Qayyim's *Shifa' al-'Alil*, devoted entirely to this subject, explains that God's decree does not bypass causes. It includes them. Water quenches thirst and fire burns, and in a theistic account these causes operate within God's will. They are still real causes. The Qur'an's account of Abraham shows that God can suspend an ordinary effect: *"We said: O fire, be coolness and safety upon Abraham"* (21:69). That a miracle is needed to stop fire burning shows that, ordinarily, fire really does burn.

In the same way, **a human decision is a real cause within a world God sustains.** God's decree includes the fact that I will choose, what I will choose, and the consequences of my choosing. That does not make the choice less mine. It makes it part of a reality that God knows and wills. Ibn Taymiyya puts it this way in his writings on decree: the servant is truly the doer of his action, and God is its creator.

## Knowing is not forcing

God's prior knowledge is knowledge of what I **will freely do**. A teacher who knows a student well may predict exactly how the student will answer an exam question. The prediction does not cause the answer. God's knowledge is perfect rather than a guess, but the same distinction applies. Knowledge follows the reality known. It does not compel it.

## The Prophet's practical answer

When the Prophet ﷺ told his Companions that each person's place in Paradise or Hell had been written, they asked: *"Shall we not then rely on what has been written and abandon work?"* He replied:

> *"Act, for each person is eased toward what he was created for."* He then recited: *"As for he who gives and fears God and believes in the best reward, We will ease him toward ease. But as for he who withholds and considers himself free of need and denies the best reward, We will ease him toward difficulty."* (Sahih al-Bukhari 4949; Sahih Muslim 2647; Quran 92:5–10)

The decree is not a script handed to us in advance. It is hidden from us, and the only way any of us meets it is by choosing and acting.

## Why send messengers?

God sent messengers, revealed commands and gave warnings. All of these presuppose that people can respond. *"Messengers bringing good news and warnings, so that people would have no argument against God after the messengers"* (4:165). A God who intended human beings to be puppets would have no reason to argue with them, persuade them and wait for their repentance.

## What remains

Islamic theology affirms both truths and admits that the full relationship between human choice and divine decree is among the questions human understanding cannot exhaust. That is not an evasion. It is a recognition that we are creatures asking about the inner workings of the Creator's will. What we can know is enough to live by. Our choices are real, we are responsible for them, and God is just: *"God does not burden a soul beyond its capacity"* (2:286).
