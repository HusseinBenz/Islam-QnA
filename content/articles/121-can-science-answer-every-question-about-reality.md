A physicist can use equations to model how a universe behaves. A further question is **why there is a universe with those laws at all**. The two questions are related, but the evidence needed to answer each is different. Keeping them apart is the key to this whole debate.

## What science does superbly

Science investigates the observable world by observation, measurement, hypothesis and testing. Its results have transformed medicine, technology and our picture of the cosmos. The Qur'an invites exactly this kind of investigation: *"Say: Travel through the land and observe how He began creation"* (29:20). It repeatedly points to the heavens, the earth, living things and the human body as signs worth studying. For centuries Muslim scholars were among the world's leading scientists ([see here](article.html?id=18)). Jim Al-Khalili's *The House of Wisdom* tells part of that story.

## Where the "conflict" story comes from

The popular narrative of an eternal war between science and religion grew largely out of European history, especially disputes between scientists and church authorities, such as the trial of Galileo in 1633. Historians of science now regard even that narrative as oversimplified. In any case, it is not a template for every religion. Islamic civilisation produced a different history, in which the study of nature was often understood as a religious act.

## Scientism: a philosophical claim in scientific dress

**Scientism** is the view that science is the only source of real knowledge, so that whatever science cannot establish is either meaningless or false. The problem is simple. **That claim cannot itself be established by science.** No experiment shows that only experiments yield knowledge. Scientism is a philosophical thesis, and by its own standard it would have to be rejected ([see sources of knowledge](article.html?id=45)).

Scientists, like everyone else, bring philosophical assumptions to their work. A physicist's expertise gives great weight to a claim **within physics**. A broader conclusion about God, meaning or morality deserves to be examined on its own reasoning, whoever draws it.

## An example

Stephen Hawking and Leonard Mlodinow write in *The Grand Design* (chapter 8): *"Because there is a law such as gravity, the universe can and will create itself from nothing."*

Look at the three layers in that sentence:

1. **The observation and model.** Physics describes how gravity and quantum fields behave.
2. **The conclusion.** The universe "creates itself from nothing."
3. **The hidden premise.** A law of gravity exists before the universe does.

The difficulty is in the third layer. A law of gravity is not nothing, and it presupposes something for gravity to act on. "Something governed by laws" is not "nothing." Even if the model were correct, the further question remains: **why do those laws and that reality exist?** Physics answers how; it does not, by itself, answer that ([see laws of nature](article.html?id=61)).

## Questions beyond the laboratory

Some questions are real, important and answerable by reasoning, but not by experiment:

- Why is there something rather than nothing?
- Why is the universe orderly and intelligible?
- Is cruelty for its own sake wrong, and why?
- What is a human life for?

Ibn Taymiyya's *Dar' Ta'arud al-'Aql wa-l-Naql* argues that reason has its own first principles, which experiment presupposes and cannot replace. Muslims hold that these deeper questions are addressed by reason and revelation together. That is a claim to be examined on its merits, just as scientism is.

## How to read big claims

When a scientist makes a sweeping statement about God or meaning, look for the **observation**, the **model** and the **conclusion**, and ask what evidence supports each step. Often the science is sound and the philosophy is not. See also [claims that science and Islam conflict](article.html?id=122) and [quantum physics and a creator](article.html?id=126).
