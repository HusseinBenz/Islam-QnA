Critics of the hadith tradition sometimes quote a story that seems to show a Companion in a bad light, and then conclude that the transmitters cannot be trusted. Hadith scholars asked exactly the same kind of question, and they had a method for answering it.

## A test case: "Visit seldom"

A popular story claims that Abu Hurayra visited the Prophet so often, hoping for food, that the Prophet told him: *"Visit seldom, and love will grow"* (*zur ghibban tazdad hubban*). The story is used to paint Abu Hurayra, the Companion who narrated the most hadith, as greedy and unreliable.

The story contains **two separate claims**: that the Prophet said these words, and that he said them because of Abu Hurayra's appetite. Each can be checked.

- **The saying.** It appears in collections such as the *Musnad* of al-Tayalisi and the *Musnad* of al-Bazzar. Al-Bazzar himself remarked, in the comment preserved in al-Haythami's *Kashf al-Astar*, that he knew of no sound chain for it from the Prophet. Al-'Uqayli, in *al-Du'afa' al-Kabir*, lists it among the reports of weak transmitters.
- **The motive.** The food explanation has even less support. It is an addition that does not appear in the better-attested versions of the saying at all.

So the story fails at the first test. The weakness was identified by the hadith critics themselves. They recorded the report precisely so that it could be examined.

## The discipline: *al-jarh wa-l-ta'dil*

*Al-jarh wa-l-ta'dil* means "disparagement and accreditation." It is the systematic recording of evidence about each transmitter's **integrity** (*'adala*) and **accuracy** (*dabt*). Critics wrote vast biographical dictionaries recording, for thousands of transmitters, when they lived, whom they met, who studied with them, and what qualified scholars said about them. Al-Dhahabi's *Siyar A'lam al-Nubala'* is one of the best known.

Critics asked two questions about any accusation:

1. **Is it well founded?** An accusation needs its own chain and its own evidence. A rival's grudge or a vague rumour does not count.
2. **Does it bear on reliability?** A person's error in a matter unrelated to transmission, or a disputed opinion, does not necessarily make their reports unreliable. Dishonesty or poor memory does.

## Rigour without favouritism

The critics were famously unsparing. Muslim records in the introduction to his *Sahih* that scholars openly declared weak those they judged weak, as a matter of religious duty. 'Ali ibn al-Madini, among the greatest critics, is reported to have been asked about his own father and to have said that he was weak in hadith.

## Reliability across theological lines

The two Sahih collections include transmitters from several theological currents: people with Shi'i leanings, Kharijite sympathisers, and people accused of Qadarite views on free will. Al-Bukhari, for example, narrates from 'Imran ibn Hittan, a known Kharijite sympathiser.

The scholars' reasoning, discussed by Ibn al-Salah and others, was that **what matters for transmission is truthfulness and accuracy**, not agreement with the critic's theology. Many were more cautious about a transmitter who actively promoted his doctrine, especially in reports that supported it. Actual judgments depended on the person and the report. Accepting truthful opponents shows the method was not simply a tool for confirming one's own side.

## What this means for a reader today

When a report seems to undermine a Companion or the Sunnah, ask:

1. Where is it recorded, and with what chain?
2. What did the hadith critics say about that chain?
3. Is the damaging part, often an explanation or motive, in the same report, or a later addition?

Often, as with "visit seldom," the critics have already done the work. See also [checking a claim about a text](article.html?id=49) and [why scholars trust the Companions](article.html?id=108).
