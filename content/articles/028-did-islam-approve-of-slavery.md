This is a hard question, and it deserves a straight answer in place of a defensive one.

## The world Islam entered

In the seventh century, slavery was universal. Rome, Persia, India, China, Africa and Arabia all practised it, and no philosophy or religion of the time had called for its abolition. A person could be enslaved through debt, kidnapping, raiding or being sold by their own family.

Islam did not introduce this institution. The question is what it did with it.

## It closed the roads in

Islam forbade enslaving a free person. In a sacred hadith, God names among those He will personally oppose on the Day of Judgment "a man who sold a free person and consumed his price" (Sahih al-Bukhari 2227). Enslavement for debt, by kidnapping, and by raiding peaceful people was abolished. Only captives taken in a lawful war remained, and even for them the Quran names two outcomes, release as a favour or release for ransom (47:4).

## It opened the roads out

No earlier law built so many exits:

- Freeing a slave was made the **expiation** for several sins, including accidental killing (4:92) and breaking an oath (5:89).
- A share of the **zakat**, the obligatory charity, is assigned to freeing slaves (9:60).
- A slave could demand a **contract to buy their freedom**, and owners were told to agree and to help with the payment (24:33).
- The Quran lists freeing a slave among the acts that define true righteousness (2:177) and calls it "the steep path" to God (90:11–13).
- The Prophet ﷺ said that whoever slaps or beats a slave must free him as atonement (Sahih Muslim 1657).

The Prophet ﷺ freed those who came into his own household, and his closest Companion, Abu Bakr, spent much of his wealth buying and freeing persecuted slaves.

## It changed the relationship

> *"Your slaves are your brothers. God has placed them in your care. So whoever has his brother under his care should feed him from what he eats and clothe him from what he wears, and not burden them with more than they can bear."* — Sahih al-Bukhari 30

He forbade people even to say "my slave", telling them to say "my young man" or "my young woman" (Sahih al-Bukhari 2552).

The results were visible. Bilal, an African former slave, became the first caller to prayer, and the caliph Umar referred to him as "our master" (Sahih al-Bukhari 3754). Zayd ibn Haritha, given the choice, preferred to stay with the Prophet ﷺ over returning home with his own father. Later, former slaves rose to be scholars, generals and even sultans. Slavery in Islamic law was never based on race.

## Why not abolish it in one day?

A world economy and the customs of war rested on slavery, and enemies went on enslaving Muslim captives. A sudden ban by one side could not have held. Islam chose the method it used with other deep-rooted evils: dry up the sources, multiply the ways out, and transform how people are treated. Muslim jurists summarised the direction in a maxim: *the Lawgiver longs for freedom.*

## An honest conclusion

Muslims did not always follow this guidance. Slave trades existed in Muslim lands for centuries, and wrongs were committed. That is a failure of people to live up to the teaching.

When the world finally agreed to abolish slavery, Muslim scholars endorsed abolition as the fulfilment of what their scripture had been urging all along. Jonathan Brown examines this whole history frankly in *Slavery and Islam*.
