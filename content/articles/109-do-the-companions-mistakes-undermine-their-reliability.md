The Battle of the Camel (656) and the Battle of Siffin (657) were real conflicts in which Companions of the Prophet fought on opposing sides. Critics ask how people who fought each other can all be trusted as transmitters. The question deserves a clear answer.

## Integrity is not infallibility

Sunni scholarship never claimed the Companions were free of error or sin. Al-Ghazali (*al-Mustasfa*), al-'Ala'i (*Jami' al-Tahsil*) and Ibn Taymiyya (*Majmu' al-Fatawa*) all distinguish the Companions' **integrity as transmitters** (*'adala*) from **infallibility** (*'isma*), which belongs to prophets alone. A Companion could make a serious mistake, in politics, judgment or personal conduct. What the scholars denied was that any Companion would **deliberately invent** a statement and attribute it to the Prophet.

The Qur'an itself anticipates conflict among believers and does not strip either side of the name:

> *"And if two groups of believers fight, make peace between them. But if one of them transgresses against the other, fight the one that transgresses until it returns to the command of God."* (Quran 49:9)

The Prophet ﷺ said of his grandson al-Hasan: *"God will reconcile through him two great groups of Muslims"* (Sahih al-Bukhari 2704). This happened in 661, when al-Hasan gave up his claim to the caliphate to end the fighting. Both groups are called Muslims. He also told 'Ammar ibn Yasir that he would be killed by *"the transgressing group"* (Sahih Muslim 2916), and 'Ammar was killed at Siffin. The sources record the conflicts, and judgments about them, openly.

## The sources record mistakes plainly

The tradition did not hide the Companions' failings:

- **Ka'b ibn Malik** stayed behind from the expedition to Tabuk without excuse. When the Prophet returned, others gave false excuses, but Ka'b told the truth about his own failure. He was ostracised for fifty days until his repentance was accepted. He narrated the whole story himself, against his own interest (Sahih al-Bukhari 4418).
- **Hatib ibn Abi Balta'a** secretly wrote to warn Quraysh of the Prophet's march on Mecca, a grave breach. The letter was intercepted. The Prophet heard his explanation and forgave him, noting that he had fought at Badr (Sahih al-Bukhari 3007).
- **A woman of Juhayna** came to the Prophet and confessed a serious sin. After the penalty, the Prophet prayed over her and said her repentance was so great that, if divided among seventy people of Medina, it would suffice them (Sahih Muslim 1696).

Such reports show two things. The community did not pretend its members were perfect. And people who were willing to tell the truth **against themselves** are hardly people who would lie about the Prophet for their own advantage.

## Every allegation needs evidence

Many accusations against particular Companions, about what someone did in a conflict or what the Prophet supposedly said about them, come through late or weak chains, sometimes from partisans of one side. Before such a report can support a judgment, it needs a reliable source ([see how to check](article.html?id=49)).

The standard has to be applied **consistently**. A reader should test an account they find persuasive by the same criteria as one they would prefer to reject.

## Repentance and the whole person

The Juhayna woman's story points to something else. How a person **responds** to their wrongdoing tells us about them as much as the wrong itself. The Companions are remembered for their sacrifices, their repentance and their honesty, alongside their mistakes. That is compatible with the Sunni presumption of their honesty in reporting ([see here](article.html?id=108)). It is not a claim that they were beyond criticism.
