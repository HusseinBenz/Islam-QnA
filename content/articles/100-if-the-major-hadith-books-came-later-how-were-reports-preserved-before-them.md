The most famous hadith collections, those of al-Bukhari (d. 870) and Muslim (d. 875), were completed more than two centuries after the Prophet. Critics sometimes infer that for two hundred years hadith floated around as unrecorded rumour. The historical record shows something different.

## A later anthology can contain earlier material

A modern anthology of medieval poems is recent, but the poems are not. The important question is not when the anthology was made but **how the compiler obtained the material**, and what evidence survives of its earlier circulation. For hadith, three kinds of evidence answer that question.

## 1. Writing from the beginning

Writing hadith began in the Prophet's lifetime ([see here](article.html?id=99)) and spread steadily:

- **'Abd Allah ibn 'Amr** kept a written collection, known as *al-Sahifa al-Sadiqa*, "the Truthful Notebook."
- **Hammam ibn Munabbih**, a student of Abu Hurayra, wrote down about 140 hadith from his teacher in the first Islamic century. The *Sahifa of Hammam* **survives in manuscript** and has been published. Its reports also appear, with the same wording and through Hammam, in the *Musannaf* of 'Abd al-Razzaq and the *Musnad* of Ahmad ibn Hanbal, decades before al-Bukhari. Comparing them allows scholars to check that the later books preserved the earlier text accurately.
- Many other Companions and Successors kept notebooks, and these are recorded by name in the early biographical literature.

Muhammad Mustafa al-A'zami's *Studies in Early Hadith Literature* (*Dirasat fi al-Hadith al-Nabawi*) documents scores of such early written collections and traces their contents into the later books.

## 2. Official compilation in the early second century

The Umayyad caliph 'Umar ibn 'Abd al-'Aziz (r. 717–720) wrote to Abu Bakr ibn Hazm, the judge of Medina, instructing him to look for the Prophet's hadith and write them down, *"for I fear the loss of knowledge and the passing away of the scholars."* Al-Bukhari records this instruction in the Book of Knowledge of his *Sahih*. Scholars such as Ibn Shihab al-Zuhri gathered hadith systematically in this period.

Large organised works followed: Malik's *Muwatta'* (mid-eighth century), the *Musannaf* of 'Abd al-Razzaq, the *Musnad* of al-Tayalisi, and many others, all well before the famous collections.

## 3. Memorisation and teaching

Arabian culture was strongly oral. People memorised long poems and genealogies, and the Companions memorised the Qur'an. Hadith were taught in circles, where a teacher recited, students repeated and wrote, and they were then examined. Students travelled long distances to hear a report directly from a living transmitter. This practice, *al-rihla fi talab al-hadith*, is described in detail in al-Khatib al-Baghdadi's works.

## The chain of transmission as a check

Because each report carries its **chain** (*isnad*), the route by which it reached a later compiler can be traced and tested. When several students of the same teacher transmit the same report independently, critics can compare their versions. That is how errors were detected ([see here](article.html?id=101)). Writing, memory and chains were not alternatives. They checked one another.

## What this does and does not show

The early records show that hadith writing and systematic transmission were well under way before the famous collections. They do not mean every report in every book is authentic. A reader assessing an individual report still needs to examine its chain, its wording and any other surviving versions. That is exactly what the classical critics did, and what modern scholars continue to do.
