Quantum physics is one of the most successful theories ever devised. It is also strange, and its strangeness is sometimes used to argue that the universe needs no cause. It is worth separating what the physics shows from what is being claimed on its behalf.

## What quantum physics does

Quantum theory often describes events in terms of **probabilities**. It may not tell you when a particular radioactive atom will decay or where a single electron will land on a screen. It tells you, with great precision, the **pattern** that will emerge over many such events. Experiments test those predicted patterns across many measurements and confirm them again and again.

Experiments on "Bell inequalities," recognised by the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics, showed that quantum systems behave in ways no theory of local hidden variables can reproduce. This is evidence about **how physical systems behave**. It is remarkable, and it is fully compatible with belief in a Creator.

## Claim 1: "Quantum events come from nothing"

When someone says particles "pop into existence from nothing," ask what *nothing* means in that explanation. In quantum field theory the "vacuum" is not nothing. It is the lowest-energy state of quantum fields that exist, obey laws, and have measurable properties. Particles that appear there arise **from** something: a physical state governed by physical laws.

That is a very different starting point from the philosophical idea of **absolute nothingness**: no fields, no laws, no space, no time, no potential of any kind. The classical argument for God concerns that question: why there is any reality at all ([see who created God?](article.html?id=60)). Quantum physics does not address it, because it always begins with something to describe.

## Claim 2: "Quantum events have no cause"

Quantum indeterminacy means that some individual outcomes are not fixed in advance by prior physical conditions. Does that mean things happen without any cause?

- **The interpretation is disputed.** Physicists disagree about what quantum theory means. Some interpretations are indeterministic. Others, such as Bohmian mechanics, are fully deterministic. Many-worlds interpretations avoid randomness in another way. Indeterminism is one reading, not an established fact about reality.
- **Unpredictable is not uncaused.** Even on indeterministic readings, quantum events occur **within** existing systems, according to laws and probabilities. An outcome that cannot be predicted is still an outcome of something.
- **The principle at stake is about beginnings.** Al-Ghazali's premise was that whatever *begins to exist* needs a cause for its existence. A probabilistic event within an existing physical system does not show that a whole universe could begin to exist with nothing behind it.

## The further question remains

Suppose a quantum model of the early universe someday succeeds in explaining how our cosmos developed from an initial state. That would be a great achievement. Even so, there would remain room to ask **why there is a physical reality, with those laws and that initial state, for the model to describe**. Ibn Taymiyya's *Dar' Ta'arud al-'Aql wa-l-Naql* argues that this kind of question belongs to reason's first principles, and that no description of the world's internal workings can answer it ([see laws of nature](article.html?id=61)).

In short, quantum physics tells us a great deal about **how** physical reality behaves at its smallest scales. Whether physical reality is **all there is**, and why it exists at all, are philosophical questions, and they need philosophical arguments.
