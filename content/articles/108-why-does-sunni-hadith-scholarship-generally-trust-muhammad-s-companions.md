A Companion (*sahabi*) is someone who met the Prophet ﷺ as a believer and died as a Muslim. Much of what later generations know about the Prophet's words and practice came through these people. Their reliability is therefore central to the Sunni case for the Sunnah.

## The Qur'an's praise

The Qur'an speaks of the first believers in terms that Sunni scholars regard as decisive:

> *"And the first forerunners among the Emigrants and the Helpers, and those who followed them with excellence, God is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him."* (Quran 9:100)

> *"God was certainly pleased with the believers when they pledged allegiance to you under the tree."* (Quran 48:18)

> *"Muhammad is the Messenger of God, and those with him are firm against the disbelievers, merciful among themselves."* (Quran 48:29)

It also praises the Emigrants who *"were expelled from their homes and their property, seeking bounty from God,"* and the Helpers who *"give preference to others over themselves, even though they are in need"* (59:8–9).

## The Prophet's words

> *"The best of people are my generation, then those who follow them, then those who follow them."* (Sahih al-Bukhari 2652)

> *"Do not revile my Companions. If one of you spent gold equal to Mount Uhud, it would not reach a handful of one of them, nor half of it."* (Sahih al-Bukhari 3673)

## The scholars' reasoning

Classical scholars gave several reasons for treating the Companions as honest transmitters (*'udul*):

- **Ibn 'Abd al-Barr**, in the introduction to *al-Isti'ab*, his dictionary of the Companions, states that their integrity is established by God's and the Prophet's praise, so their individual characters need not be investigated as later transmitters' were.
- **Al-Juwayni**, in *al-Burhan*, connects their standing with their role as the transmitters of the religion. If they were not trusted, the transmission of the Qur'an and Sunnah would have no foundation.
- **Al-Ghazali**, in *al-Mustasfa*, points to the praise in scripture and reports, and to the sacrifices they made: emigration, loss of property, and risking their lives for the faith.
- **Al-'Ala'i**, in *Jami' al-Tahsil*, and **Ibn Taymiyya**, in his fatwas, describe the Companions' integrity as a settled Sunni position.

## What the presumption does and does not mean

**It concerns honesty in transmission, not infallibility.** Sunni scholars hold that Companions could make mistakes, misremember, disagree with each other and even sin ([see here](article.html?id=109)). What the scholars denied was that any of them would **deliberately lie** about the Prophet.

**The chain must still be sound.** The presumption applies to the Companion. It says nothing about the later people who claim to transmit from him. A report attributed to a Companion through a weak or broken chain is still weak ([see here](article.html?id=49)).

**The Companions themselves checked one another.** When a grandmother asked Abu Bakr for a share of inheritance, he asked whether anyone knew the Prophet's ruling. He accepted it only after a second Companion confirmed al-Mughira's report (Sunan Abi Dawud 2894). 'Umar asked Abu Musa to bring a witness for a report about seeking permission to enter (Sahih al-Bukhari 6245). This was not distrust of their honesty. It was care for accuracy.

## Other traditions

Other Islamic traditions, notably Shi'i scholarship, have assessed individual Companions differently, especially in relation to the political disputes after the Prophet's death. That disagreement is real and has a long history. The Sunni position rests on the evidence above, and on the observation that the Companions' reports, compared with one another, show a high degree of consistency.
