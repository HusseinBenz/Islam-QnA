The accounts name people from ʿUkl or ʿUrayna. After falling ill near Medina, they are reported to have been sent to a herd of camels and told to use camel milk and urine. That detail, which the book mentions when discussing how the hadith is debated, records a practice in a particular historical setting. Establishing a treatment’s safety and effectiveness requires clinical evidence.

The accounts then say the men killed a herdsman and took the herd. They were captured and subjected to a punishment that included mutilation and death. One version in Muslim presents part of the punishment as retaliation for what they had done to the herdsmen.

The report is also discussed in connection with Qur’an 5:33–34, a passage classical jurists applied to violent banditry. Other reports record a general prohibition of mutilation. These texts raise a legal and historical question about the circumstances, scope and relation of the different rulings. The book names the episode in its outline. The account here comes from the early reports themselves.
