This report appears in the most reliable hadith collections and is often quoted in isolation. Read in full, it raises legal and historical questions that Muslim scholars discussed openly.

## What the reports say

Anas ibn Malik narrates that a group of men from the tribes of 'Ukl and 'Urayna came to Medina and accepted Islam. The climate did not suit them and they fell ill. The Prophet ﷺ sent them out to the camels kept for charity, outside the city, and told them to drink the camels' milk and urine. They recovered.

Then they **killed the Prophet's herdsman, drove off the camels, and renounced Islam**. A search party caught them. Their hands and feet were cut off, their eyes were branded, and they were left in the lava field outside Medina until they died (Sahih al-Bukhari 233, 4192; Sahih Muslim 1671).

A version in Sahih Muslim adds a crucial detail. Anas said: *"The Prophet put out their eyes only because they had put out the eyes of the herdsmen"* (Sahih Muslim 1671h). On this report, the punishment was **retaliation in kind** (*qisas*) for what they had done to the herdsman.

One of the transmitters, Abu Qilaba, summed up their crimes: they stole, they killed, they renounced faith after belief, and they waged war against God and His Messenger (Sahih al-Bukhari 233).

## The Qur'an on armed robbery

The incident is discussed in connection with this verse:

> *"The recompense of those who wage war against God and His Messenger and strive to spread corruption in the land is that they be killed or crucified, or that their hands and feet be cut off from opposite sides, or that they be exiled from the land... Except for those who repent before you overpower them."* (Quran 5:33–34)

Classical jurists applied this verse, the law of *hiraba*, to violent banditry: armed robbery, terrorising travellers, killing and plundering. Al-Tabari records that some early scholars held the verse was revealed about the 'Urayna case, while others held it was revealed more generally. Either way, jurists treated the verse as defining and **limiting** the penalties for such crimes. They also gave the ruler discretion, and they held that the penalties lapse if the offender repents before capture.

## Mutilation and its prohibition

Other reports record that the Prophet ﷺ prohibited mutilation (*muthla*) in general (Sahih al-Bukhari 2474, 5516). How do these fit together? Ibn Hajar discusses the views in *Fath al-Bari*:

- that the punishment was **retaliation** for mutilating the herdsman, as the report in Muslim states;
- that it **preceded** the revelation of 5:33 and the general prohibition of mutilation, which then set the limits;
- or both.

These texts raise a genuine legal and historical question about the circumstances, the scope of each ruling and how they relate. The scholars did not hide that question.

## The camel urine

The report says the men were told to drink camel milk and urine, and some classical jurists debated whether the urine of animals whose meat is eaten is ritually pure. The detail records a practice in a particular historical setting. **It is not medical advice.** Establishing that a treatment is safe and effective requires clinical evidence. The World Health Organization has advised against drinking raw camel milk or camel urine because of the risk of infection, including MERS coronavirus. Islamic law itself forbids exposing oneself to harm.

## Reading it in context

The incident involved men who were welcomed, cared for and healed, and who then murdered their caregiver, mutilated him and stole public property. The punishment was severe, and a reader may still find it hard. But the full reports, the Qur'anic law that followed and the jurists' discussion show a case being reasoned about, not a model of indiscriminate cruelty.
