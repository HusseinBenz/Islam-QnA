Readers who know the Bible are often struck by how many familiar figures appear in the Qur'an: Adam, Noah, Abraham, Joseph, Moses, David, Solomon, Jonah, Mary and Jesus. Some conclude that Muhammad must have borrowed the stories. The resemblance is real. What it shows is another matter.

## Continuity is the Qur'an's own claim

The Qur'an does not hide the overlap. It announces it:

> *"He has sent down upon you the Book in truth, confirming what was before it, and He sent down the Torah and the Gospel before."* (Quran 3:3)

> *"Indeed, this is in the former scriptures, the scriptures of Abraham and Moses."* (Quran 87:18–19)

If one God sent prophets throughout history, we should **expect** their messages and stories to overlap. On the Qur'an's own account, shared material is evidence of a shared source, not of copying.

## But the Qur'an also differs

The Qur'an is not a summary of earlier texts. It retells episodes with different details, purposes and emphases, and sometimes it corrects what it regards as later distortions. It calls itself *muhaymin*, a "guardian" or "criterion," over earlier scripture (5:48). Some examples:

- **Aaron and the golden calf.** In Exodus 32, Aaron makes the calf. In the Qur'an, a man called al-Samiri makes it, and Aaron tries to stop the people (20:85–94).
- **Solomon.** The Qur'an insists that *"Solomon did not disbelieve"* (2:102), against accounts that he turned to idolatry in old age (1 Kings 11).
- **The prophets' character.** The Qur'an consistently presents prophets as upright, without the moral failings attributed to some of them in other sources.
- **Material not found in the Bible**, such as the story of Moses and al-Khidr (18:60–82), the people of the Cave told in its own way, Hud and Salih, and Mary's birth and upbringing told in detail (3:35–37).

Ibn Taymiyya's *al-Jawab al-Sahih* and Rahmatullah Kairanawi's *Izhar al-Haqq* compare the accounts in detail.

## "You were not there"

After telling some of these stories, the Qur'an turns directly to Muhammad:

> *"That is from the news of the unseen which We reveal to you. You did not know it, neither you nor your people, before this."* (Quran 11:49), after the story of Noah.

> *"And you were not on the western side when We decreed the matter to Moses, nor were you among the witnesses."* (Quran 28:44)

> *"You did not recite any scripture before it, nor did you write one with your right hand."* (Quran 29:48)

Ibn Kathir explains these verses as a direct challenge to the audience. Muhammad's contemporaries knew his life. They knew he had no teacher of scripture and could not read the earlier books.

## The obvious objection, and the Qur'an's reply

Meccan opponents raised the borrowing theory themselves: *"We know that they say, 'It is only a human being who teaches him.'"* The Qur'an answered: *"The tongue of the one they refer to is foreign, and this is a clear Arabic tongue"* (16:103). Whoever they had in mind spoke little Arabic, and could not have been the source of a text whose power lies in its Arabic.

## How to think about it

Historical inquiry can legitimately ask what stories circulated orally in seventh-century Arabia and compare the form of each text. That is worth doing. But **the presence of a parallel, by itself, cannot establish how a story reached a particular speaker.** The real question is how someone traditionally described as unlettered delivered narratives with such detail, rhetorical structure, consistency and moral focus. They were woven into a scripture that also issues a [literary challenge](article.html?id=75) no one met. See also [is Muhammad mentioned in the Bible?](article.html?id=21)
