This is one of the most difficult rulings to discuss, and it deserves care. The aim here is to explain what the sources say, how classical jurists reasoned, and where the real questions lie.

## The Qur'an and the Sunnah

The Qur'an says:

> *"The woman and the man guilty of zina, flog each of them with a hundred lashes."* (Quran 24:2)

Classical Sunni jurists, across all four schools, held that the Sunnah **specifies the scope** of this verse. The flogging applies to a person who has never been lawfully married. A person who is *muhsan*, meaning one who is free, adult, sane and has consummated a lawful marriage, is liable to stoning. Their evidence includes:

- the cases of **Ma'iz ibn Malik** and **the woman of Ghamid**, both of whom confessed (Sahih Muslim 1695);
- the case of **a woman of Juhayna** (Sahih Muslim 1696);
- **'Umar ibn al-Khattab's** public statement from the pulpit that stoning was part of what God revealed and that the Prophet had applied it (Sahih al-Bukhari 6829; Sahih Muslim 1691).

That the Sunnah can specify a Qur'anic rule is a general principle in Sunni legal theory ([see here](article.html?id=94)). Ibn 'Abd al-Barr (*al-Tamhid*) and Ibn Battal (*Sharh Sahih al-Bukhari*) record that the only early dissent came from Kharijite and some Mu'tazilite thinkers, who rejected stoning because it is not in the Qur'an. Ibn Qudama sets out the conditions in detail in *al-Mughni*.

## The objection from Qur'an 4:25

Qur'an 4:25 says that enslaved women who commit *zina* receive *"half the punishment of the muhsanat."* A death penalty cannot be halved, so the objection runs that the penalty for married women must be the divisible one, flogging.

The classical reply looks at the verse's own vocabulary. Earlier in the same verse, *muhsanat* is contrasted with enslaved women. It means **free women**, as in *"whoever cannot afford to marry the free believing women (al-muhsanat)."* So "half the punishment of the *muhsanat*" means half the punishment of free women in the divisible case: fifty lashes instead of a hundred. On this reading the verse does not address stoning at all.

## Evidence: designed to make conviction rare

Classical law surrounded this penalty with requirements that made it extremely difficult to apply:

- **Four witnesses** of upright character must testify to having seen the act itself, explicitly (24:4, 24:13). This is a standard that ordinary circumstances almost never meet.
- Anyone who accuses without four such witnesses is **himself punished** with eighty lashes for false accusation (24:4).
- A **confession** must be made voluntarily and repeatedly, and may be **withdrawn** at any time, even during the punishment.
- Any genuine **doubt** (*shubha*) averts the penalty.

In practice, the cases recorded in the Prophet's time came from **voluntary confession**, and the Prophet discouraged it. When Ma'iz came to confess, the Prophet turned away from him repeatedly, asked whether he was mad or drunk, and suggested that he might only have kissed or touched (Sahih Muslim 1695; Sahih al-Bukhari 6824). He told the man who had urged Ma'iz to confess: *"If you had covered him with your garment, it would have been better for you"* (Sunan Abi Dawud 4377).

Private sins were to be **concealed and repented of**, not exposed. Jurists did discuss openly committed offences differently, and they held that only a legitimate court could apply any such penalty, never an individual or a mob.

## Why so severe?

Classical scholars read the severity as a **deterrent** that marks the gravity of betraying a marriage and its effect on lineage, family and trust. They compared it with the grave penalties of 5:33 for armed robbery. The heavy evidentiary barrier meant that the law's main effect was to declare a moral boundary, while very few people were ever actually punished.

## The ethical question remains

This explanation describes historical courts and legal theory. It explains the reasoning behind the rule, but it does not dissolve the ethical question. Many people, including many Muslims, find the penalty extremely hard, and its severity is central to the modern debate. Saying so honestly is part of understanding the issue.
