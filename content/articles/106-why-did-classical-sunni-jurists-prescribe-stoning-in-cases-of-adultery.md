question: Why did classical Sunni jurists prescribe stoning in cases of adultery?

The starting point is the source question. Quran 24:2 names flogging for *zinā*, or unlawful sexual intercourse. Classical Sunni jurists cited several hadith and rulings attributed to the early caliphs for a judicial penalty of stoning for a legally qualified category tied to marriage history. Al-Sayyid treats the Sunnah as able to specify the scope of a Quranic rule. He reads the severity of the penalty as a deterrent and compares it with the grave penalties in Quran 5:33. Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr and Ibn Battal also record dissent from some early Kharijite and Muʿtazilite thinkers.

**What about Quran 4:25?** The verse gives enslaved women half of the penalty of the *muḥṣanāt*. The objection is that a death penalty cannot be halved. The book reads *muḥṣanāt* here in light of the contrast with enslaved women earlier in the verse. On the classical reading it cites, the comparison is with free women subject to the divisible penalty in 24:2.

The author points to demanding evidentiary requirements, to historical reports of voluntary confession, and to the preference for privacy over public accusation in cases of private conduct. He allows that jurists discuss openly committed offenses differently. This is an account of historical courts and legal theory. It explains the reasoning behind the rule and why its severity remains central to the ethical debate.
