Readers new to the Qur'an are sometimes surprised to find God speaking as "We":

> *"Indeed, it is We who sent down the Reminder, and indeed, We will be its guardian."* (Quran 15:9)

> *"Indeed, We sent it down on the Night of Decree."* (Quran 97:1)

Does this suggest more than one God? The Qur'an's own answer, and the answer of Arabic grammar, is no.

## The plural of majesty

Arabic, like many languages, allows a single speaker of high rank to refer to themselves in the plural. Grammarians call it *nun al-'azama*, the "plural of majesty." English has the "royal we," used historically by monarchs, and other languages have similar forms. The plural here expresses greatness, not number.

Ibn Taymiyya addressed this directly. Some Christians of his time cited these plurals as evidence for the Trinity. In his creed treatise *al-Tadmuriyya* (in *Majmu' al-Fatawa*, vol. 3), and in his reply to Christian arguments, *al-Jawab al-Sahih*, he explained that Arabic uses *innā* and *naḥnu* ("indeed We," "We") for a single speaker of greatness. Such a speaker may also act through those who serve him, as God sends revelation through the angel Gabriel. A word whose meaning is open to more than one reading is understood by the verses that state the matter plainly.

## What the Qur'an says plainly

Where the question is how many gods there are, the Qur'an speaks in the singular and leaves no room for doubt:

> *"Say: He is God, One."* (Quran 112:1)

> *"Your God is one God. There is no god except Him, the Most Merciful, the Especially Merciful."* (Quran 2:163)

> *"Indeed, I am God. There is no god except Me, so worship Me."* (Quran 20:14)

It addresses the Trinity by name: *"Do not say 'Three.' Desist; it is better for you. God is only one God"* (4:171).

The same Qur'an often moves between "I," "We" and "He" when referring to God, sometimes in a single passage. Arabic rhetoricians call this *iltifat* (a change of person). They regarded it as a mark of eloquence that keeps the listener alert, not as a change in who is speaking. A text that so insistently declares God one would be strangely inconsistent if its plurals meant several gods. The plural of majesty is the natural reading.

## How to read a verse: clear and ambiguous

The Qur'an itself describes a method for this:

> *"It is He who has sent down to you the Book. In it are verses that are precise. They are the foundation of the Book. And others are ambiguous. As for those in whose hearts is deviation, they follow what is ambiguous, seeking discord."* (Quran 3:7)

Ibn Kathir explains, in his commentary on this verse, that the clear (*muhkam*) verses are the reference point. A verse that could at first glance bear more than one meaning is read in light of those whose meaning is plain. His example is Christian disputants who cited the Qur'an's description of Jesus as *"a spirit from Him"* (4:171) while leaving aside verses such as *"He is only a servant upon whom We bestowed favour"* (43:59). Citing "We" as evidence for plurality, while ignoring the many verses that declare God's oneness, follows the same pattern of taking the ambiguous and leaving the clear.

That method is useful well beyond this question. Whenever a claim rests on one verse read in isolation, ask what the Qur'an says about the same subject where it speaks most clearly.
