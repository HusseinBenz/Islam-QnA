Anyone claiming to speak for God is either telling the truth, deceiving others, or sincerely mistaken. A person's conduct, especially when no one would blame them for acting otherwise, tells us a great deal about which of these is likely. Here are four episodes from the most reliable sources.

## 1. The eclipse he refused to use

On the day the Prophet's infant son Ibrahim died, the sun was eclipsed. People said it had happened because of Ibrahim's death. For a man who wanted to inflate his own status, this was a perfect opportunity. He had only to stay silent. Instead the Prophet ﷺ stood and corrected them:

> *"The sun and the moon do not eclipse for the death or life of anyone. They are two of the signs of God. When you see them, pray and call upon God."* (Sahih al-Bukhari 1043; Sahih Muslim 901)

He then led the eclipse prayer and urged people to seek forgiveness and give in charity. A man inventing a religion does not usually turn down a ready-made miracle.

## 2. His reputation for honesty, even among enemies

When he first proclaimed his message publicly, he climbed Mount Safa and called the clans of Quraysh. He asked: *"If I told you that cavalry were in the valley about to attack you, would you believe me?"* They answered: *"We have never known you to lie"* (Sahih al-Bukhari 4971; Sahih Muslim 208).

Years later, the Byzantine emperor Heraclius questioned Abu Sufyan, then the Prophet's bitterest enemy, about him. Asked whether Muhammad had ever been accused of lying before his claim, Abu Sufyan admitted he had not. Heraclius reasoned: *"He would not refrain from lying about people and then lie about God"* (Sahih al-Bukhari 7).

## 3. A month without revelation

When a slander spread in Medina against his wife 'A'isha, the Prophet was under intense pressure. If he were composing revelation himself, a quick verse clearing her would have ended the crisis. Instead, for about a month no revelation came. He consulted his Companions, asked 'A'isha directly, and waited. 'A'isha herself narrates the anguish of that month. Only then were the verses declaring her innocence revealed (Sahih al-Bukhari 2661; Quran 24:11–20).

## 4. A Qur'an that corrects him

The Qur'an he recited publicly contains passages that rebuke or correct him:

- *"He frowned and turned away because the blind man came to him"* (80:1–3), about his turning from a poor blind man while speaking to Meccan leaders.
- *"May God pardon you; why did you give them leave?"* (9:43).
- On the captives of Badr: *"It is not for a prophet to have captives until he has thoroughly subdued the land"* (8:67).
- *"O Prophet, why do you forbid what God has made lawful for you?"* (66:1).
- *"And you concealed within yourself what God would disclose, and you feared the people, while God has more right that you fear Him"* (33:37).

'A'isha said that if the Prophet had concealed anything of the revelation, he would have concealed that last verse (Sahih Muslim 177). Someone writing scripture to serve himself has little reason to include his own reproof.

## How scholars weighed this evidence

Al-Ghazali, in *al-Munqidh min al-Dalal*, compared knowing a prophet to knowing that someone is a skilled physician. You may not witness the moment of his training, but you recognise his skill by observing his treatment of many patients over time. Certainty comes from **many indications together**, not from any single one. Ibn Taymiyya, in *Sharh al-'Aqida al-Asfahaniyya* and *al-Nubuwwat*, and Ibn Abi al-'Izz in his commentary on al-Tahawi's creed, similarly argued that a prophet's character, the content of his message and the course of his life are themselves evidence. They are not merely background to the miracles.

Character is not proof on its own. A sincere person could be mistaken. That is why these reports belong beside the other evidence: [the Qur'an's literary challenge](article.html?id=75), its [prophecies](article.html?id=76), the [teachings he conveyed](article.html?id=78) and the [reported miracles](article.html?id=79). The weight each reader gives to these strands may differ. Together they make a cumulative case.
