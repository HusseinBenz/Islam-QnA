In the news, "Sharia" usually appears beside the word "punishment". For Muslims it means something much larger and much gentler.

## The word and its scope

In Arabic, a *shari'a* is a path leading to a watering place, which in the desert is the path to life. In religion it means the way God has laid down for people to live.

Most of that way concerns things no court will ever handle: how to pray and fast, to tell the truth, to honour parents, to give to the poor, to keep promises, to be clean, to treat a spouse well, to be kind to animals. When a Muslim prays, gives charity or refuses a bribe, they are following the Sharia.

## What it is for

Classical scholars, notably al-Ghazali and later al-Shatibi in *al-Muwafaqat*, studied all its rulings and concluded that they serve five essential goods: the protection of **religion, life, intellect, family and property**. Any reading of the law that destroys these has misread it.

Ibn al-Qayyim put it memorably in *I'lam al-Muwaqqi'in*:

> *"The Sharia is built on wisdom and on the welfare of people in this life and the next. It is all justice, all mercy, all benefit and all wisdom. Any matter that departs from justice to injustice, from mercy to its opposite, from benefit to harm, or from wisdom to folly is not part of the Sharia, even if it has been read into it by interpretation."*

## Sharia and fiqh

The Sharia is God's guidance. *Fiqh* is the human effort to understand and apply it. That effort has produced several schools of law which agree on the foundations and differ, respectfully, on many details. Scholars have always recognised that rulings based on custom and circumstance can change as circumstances change.

## What about the punishments?

A small number of fixed penalties (*hudud*) exist for serious crimes such as theft and adultery. They come with conditions that make them very difficult to apply:

- Adultery requires **four eyewitnesses to the act itself** (Quran 24:4), a standard almost impossible to meet. A false accuser is himself punished.
- A long-established legal maxim says: **avert the fixed punishments wherever there is doubt.**
- When a man came to the Prophet ﷺ confessing adultery, the Prophet turned away from him again and again, giving him every chance to withdraw (Sahih Muslim 1695).
- The caliph Umar suspended the penalty for theft during a year of famine, because hungry people cannot be treated as thieves.

Jonathan Brown shows in *Misquoting Muhammad* that across Islamic history these penalties were carried out rarely. Their main role was to declare how seriously the law regards certain wrongs. They also presuppose a just society that has first met people's needs.

## Sharia and non-Muslims

Classical Islamic law did not impose its religious rules on other faiths. Jewish and Christian communities under Muslim rule kept their own courts and family laws.

As for Muslims living in non-Muslim countries, the Sharia itself commands: "O you who believe, fulfil your contracts" (Quran 5:1). Keeping the law of the land in which one has agreed to live is part of that obligation.

## In short

For a Muslim, the Sharia is the path to God in daily life. Its measure, in the words of its own scholars, is justice, mercy and the good of human beings.
