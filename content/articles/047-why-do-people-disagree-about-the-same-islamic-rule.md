People value freedom, stability, material security, desire, family, and achievement. They can agree that these matter yet give them very different weight. Al-Sayyid puts living in devotion to God at the center of the Islamic account. He contrasts that with an outlook in which individual choice or well-being has final authority. A person may hold a mixture of these views.

This difference can surface in debates about historic religious taxes or the treatment of apostasy. Ask which part of the rule is disputed. The question may concern what a text actually says, which conditions jurists gave, or what kind of authority a religious command can have. Identifying the issue makes a more careful answer possible. It also leaves room to ask about harm, fairness, and historical practice.
