Two people look at the same Islamic ruling. One finds it wise, the other finds it unjust. Both may be intelligent and sincere. Very often they are not disagreeing about the ruling at all. They are disagreeing about something underneath it.

## Different priorities

Almost everyone values freedom, security, family, prosperity, pleasure and achievement. People differ in how they **rank** them and in what has the final word when they conflict.

The Qur'an places one value at the centre: *"I did not create jinn and humankind except to worship Me"* (51:56). It describes a life organised around that purpose: *"Say: my prayer, my sacrifice, my living and my dying are for God, Lord of the worlds"* (6:162). It also describes the alternative: *"Have you seen the one who takes his own desire as his god?"* (45:23).

A widespread modern outlook places **individual choice or well-being** at the centre instead. On that view, a law is justified mainly if it protects a person's ability to live as they choose without harming others. Many people, Muslims included, hold a mixture of both outlooks without noticing.

If one person judges a ruling by "does it help people live in devotion to God?" and another by "does it maximise individual choice?", they will reach different verdicts on the same text. Arguing about the ruling alone will go in circles.

## What Islamic law says it protects

Classical Muslim jurists did not see God's law as arbitrary. Al-Shatibi's *al-Muwafaqat* systematised the view that the law serves the welfare of people in this life and the next, and that its rulings protect five essentials: **religion, life, intellect, lineage and property**. On this view devotion to God and human welfare are not opposites. The law serves both, but it ranks them in its own order.

Ibn al-Qayyim, in *I'lam al-Muwaqqi'in*, devotes a well-known chapter to how **legal opinions (*fatwas*) change with changes of time, place, circumstance and custom**. Fixed rulings remain fixed, but their application depends on facts. Some disagreements are about those facts, not about the rule.

## Find the level of the dispute

When a debate starts, for example about apostasy, religious taxes or inheritance, it helps to ask which of these is in question:

1. **Authenticity.** Is the text or report established? (See [how hadith are judged](article.html?id=101).)
2. **Meaning.** What does it actually say, read in context and alongside related texts?
3. **Conditions and application.** What conditions did jurists attach, and did they apply them as some imagine? (See, for example, [apostasy and "no compulsion"](article.html?id=107).)
4. **Authority.** Does a command from God have authority over our own preferences at all?

The fourth question is the deepest, and it goes back to the foundations: [is the Qur'an from God?](article.html?id=16) If it is, a believer reasons *within* its guidance. If someone doubts that, the conversation needs to begin there, not with a particular ruling.

## Room for honest questions

Identifying the level of a dispute does not end the discussion. It makes the discussion possible. It still leaves room to ask about harm, fairness, historical practice and how a ruling was really applied. Those are legitimate questions, and Muslim jurists asked them too.
