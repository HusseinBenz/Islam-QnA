The question sounds like a contradiction. If God lacks nothing, why would He ask anything of us? Isn't asking for worship a sign of needing it? The Qur'an addresses this directly, and its answer turns the question around.

## Two questions, not one

**Why create at all?** The Qur'an insists creation is not idle: *"We did not create the heavens and the earth and what is between them in play"* (21:16). *"Did you think We created you without purpose?"* (23:115). The order and variety of the world make purposeful creation a more fitting reading than a pointless act ([see here](article.html?id=58)). Part of that purpose is that God makes Himself known. He does this through reflection on the world, through human experience and conscience, and through revelation.

**Why ask for worship?** This is the second question, and it rests on a distinction between God and creatures.

## God is self-sufficient, and we are not

> *"O people, you are the ones in need of God, while God is the Free of need, the Praiseworthy."* (Quran 35:15)

> *"If you disbelieve, God is free of need of you, yet He does not approve disbelief for His servants. And if you are grateful, He approves it for you."* (Quran 39:7)

Verse 39:7 holds both ideas together. God is **independent** of people, **and** He cares how they respond, for their sake. A hadith in which the Prophet reports God's words makes the point unmistakable:

> *"O My servants, you will never be able to harm Me, nor benefit Me. O My servants, if the first and last of you, humans and jinn, were as pious as the most pious heart among you, that would not add anything to My dominion. And if they were as wicked as the most wicked heart among you, that would not reduce anything from My dominion... O My servants, it is only your deeds that I record for you and then repay you for."* (Sahih Muslim 2577)

Worship changes nothing in God. It changes the worshipper.

## What worship is

In Islamic usage, worship (*'ibada*) is much broader than ritual. Ibn Taymiyya's short treatise *al-'Ubudiyya* defines it as a comprehensive name for everything God loves and is pleased with, in words and deeds, inward and outward. It includes prayer and fasting, and also honesty, kindness to parents, keeping promises, mercy to animals, and love, trust, fear and hope directed to God.

At its core, worship is a dependent creature **acknowledging reality**: that it did not make itself, that everything it has was given, and that it is answerable for what it does. Gratitude to a benefactor is not something the benefactor needs. It is what honesty looks like on the part of the one who received.

## Why it is good for us

Ibn Taymiyya argues in *al-'Ubudiyya* that the human heart is made for God. It cannot find lasting rest in anything else, because everything else is limited, changing and itself dependent. A person who does not worship God does not stop worshipping. He gives his devotion to money, status, desire or other people, all of which can be lost. The Qur'an describes this: *"Have you seen the one who takes his desire as his god?"* (45:23). Worship of God frees a person from servitude to everything less.

> *"I did not create jinn and humankind except to worship Me. I do not want from them any provision, nor do I want them to feed Me. Indeed, God is the Provider, the Possessor of strength, the Firm."* (Quran 51:56–58)

Notice how the verse explains itself. The purpose of creation is worship, and the very next sentence denies that God gains anything from it.

## Worship and accountability

If God is the source of life and guidance, then how a person freely responds matters. A gift can be received with thanks or with contempt, and the difference reveals something real about the receiver. The Qur'an places human beings in a world answerable to God (21:16–23), and worship is the response that fits the relationship.
