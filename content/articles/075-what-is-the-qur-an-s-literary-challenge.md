The Qur’an challenges people to produce a text like it (17:88), ten chapters (11:13), or even one chapter (2:23–24). For a reader unfamiliar with Arabic, the question is what “like it” means. A translation can convey a passage’s meaning. Evaluating the literary challenge also involves the Arabic composition, sound, rhetoric and relation between form and message.

Al-Sayyid places the challenge in an oral culture that prized powerful speech and poetry. He argues that Muhammad’s opponents had both the skills and the incentives to answer it, yet produced no response that the tradition recognizes as comparable. He also points to the traditional description of Muhammad as unlettered (see 29:48).

Literary force is assessed through language and deliberate comparison, and judgments of likeness depend on the criteria used. The book understands the challenge as one reason to consider the Qur’an’s claim of revelation.
