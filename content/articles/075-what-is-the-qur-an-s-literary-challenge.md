The Qur'an makes a claim that few books make about themselves. It says that it cannot be imitated, and it invites anyone who doubts this to try.

## The challenge, in stages

> *"Say: If mankind and the jinn gathered together to produce the like of this Qur'an, they could not produce its like, even if they supported one another."* (Quran 17:88)

> *"Or do they say, 'He invented it'? Say: Then bring ten chapters like it, invented, and call upon whomever you can besides God, if you are truthful."* (Quran 11:13)

> *"And if you are in doubt about what We have sent down upon Our servant, then produce a chapter like it, and call your witnesses besides God, if you are truthful. But if you do not, and you will never do so, then fear the Fire."* (Quran 2:23–24)

The shortest chapter of the Qur'an, *al-Kawthar*, is three lines long.

## Why the setting matters

The challenge was issued to seventh-century Arabs, a people for whom poetry and eloquent speech were the highest arts. Poets were the celebrities, historians and propagandists of their tribes. Annual fairs such as 'Ukaz held poetry contests, and a skilled verse could make or ruin a reputation.

Muhammad's opponents had **the skill** to answer the challenge, and they had **every motive**. Answering it would have ended his movement without a war. They chose boycotts, persecution and eventually years of fighting instead. The tradition records no response that they themselves recognised as comparable.

## What the opponents said

The early biography of Ibn Ishaq, as edited by Ibn Hisham, and al-Bayhaqi's *Dala'il al-Nubuwwa* preserve the reaction of al-Walid ibn al-Mughira, one of the most respected judges of Arabic speech in Mecca and a determined opponent. Asked by Quraysh what they should call the Qur'an, he rejected each label in turn. It was not poetry, because he knew every form of poetry. It was not the murmuring of a soothsayer, and it was not the speech of a madman. In the end the leaders settled on calling it "magic" that separates a man from his family. The Qur'an describes this deliberation: *"He thought and deliberated... then he said, 'This is nothing but magic handed down'"* (74:18–24).

## What "like it" means

For a reader without Arabic, the key question is what would count as "like it." A translation can convey the meaning of a passage. The challenge concerns the **Arabic composition** as a whole:

- Its form is neither poetry nor ordinary prose. It is a distinctive style with its own rhythm and rhyme that Arabic critics recognised as new.
- Sound, structure and meaning are tightly bound together.
- It sustains this quality across many subjects: law, narrative, argument, description of nature and of the Hereafter.
- Its effect on listeners was immediate and powerful, and that was why its opponents tried to stop people hearing it (41:26).

Muslim scholars wrote a whole literature analysing this. Al-Khattabi, al-Rummani and al-Baqillani wrote on the Qur'an's inimitability (*i'jaz*). 'Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani's *Dala'il al-I'jaz* developed a theory of meaning in composition that is still studied by linguists. In the modern period, Muhammad Abdullah Draz's *al-Naba' al-'Azim* (translated as *The Qur'an: An Eternal Challenge*) gives a clear account.

## The Prophet's background

The Qur'an adds a further point: *"You did not recite any scripture before it, nor did you write one with your right hand. Otherwise the falsifiers would have had cause for doubt"* (29:48). The tradition describes Muhammad as *ummi*, unlettered (7:157). He had no reputation as a poet or orator before the age of forty, and his own recorded sayings (the hadith) are noticeably different in style from the Qur'an.

## How to weigh the challenge

Literary excellence is assessed through language, comparison and the judgment of those who know the language best. Criteria of "likeness" can always be debated. Muslims see the challenge as one strong reason to take the Qur'an's claim of divine origin seriously. It is one strand alongside [the Prophet's character](article.html?id=74), [the Qur'an's predictions](article.html?id=76) and the coherence of its teaching.
